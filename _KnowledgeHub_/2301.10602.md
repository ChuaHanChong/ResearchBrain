# DreamWaQ: Learning Robust Quadrupedal Locomotion With Implicit Terrain Imagination via Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KAIST developed DreamWaQ, a deep reinforcement learning framework enabling quadrupedal robots to achieve robust locomotion on challenging terrains using only proprioceptive sensors. This approach implicitly infers terrain properties and jointly learns a robust policy, demonstrating near-oracle performance in simulation and successfully navigating long-distance, diverse outdoor environments with a Unitree A1 robot.

## Problem

- Existing quadrupedal robot locomotion methods relying on exteroceptive sensors (cameras, LiDAR) are unreliable in adverse real-world conditions like bad weather or poor lighting, and can misinterpret terrain.
- Prior proprioception-only locomotion methods show limited robustness for long-distance operations on challenging and varied terrains due to accumulated estimation errors and high uncertainties.
- There is a 'representation learning bottleneck' where the policy needs to understand terrain properties for adaptation, but also needs to walk effectively to explore and learn these properties.

## Method

- DreamWaQ proposes an asymmetric actor-critic deep reinforcement learning framework where the policy uses temporal proprioceptive observations, estimated body velocity, and a latent context vector.
- A Context-Aided Estimator Network (CENet) is introduced, which jointly estimates body linear velocity and infers a latent environmental context using a Beta-VAE from proprioceptive data.
- Adaptive Bootstrapping (AdaBoot) dynamically adjusts the probability of using CENet's estimates as policy input during training based on the stability of episodic rewards, enhancing robust learning and generalization.

## Results

- DreamWaQ achieved higher mean episodic rewards in simulation, closely matching the performance of an 'oracle' policy with direct terrain access.
- The framework significantly reduced Absolute Tracking Error (ATE) for velocities and improved robustness to external disturbances, withstanding push velocities of 1.121 "m/s and achieving a 95.23% survival rate in random walks.
- The Unitree A1 robot, controlled by DreamWaQ, successfully navigated challenging real-world outdoor courses at KAIST, including 430m of unstructured campus yard (mud, stairs) and 465m of hiking track (gravel, slopes), demonstrating adaptive gaits and robust recovery.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning a robust locomotion policy can be achieved by implicitly inferring terrain properties from proprioceptive sensors, overcoming the 'representation learning bottleneck'.
> - An asymmetric actor-critic architecture, where the critic has access to privileged information during training, can effectively guide a policy trained with limited, realistic sensor inputs.
> - Adaptive bootstrapping (AdaBoot) of estimator outputs during training prevents reliance on noisy early estimates, improving overall policy robustness and generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of quadrupedal robot locomotion is rapidly advancing, driven by the need for robots that can traverse complex, unstructured environments inaccessible to wheeled robots. Traditionally, controlling quadrupedal robots involved complex model-based pipelines, requiring precise modeling and extensive human effort for parameter tuning. These conventional methods often relied on linearized models, limiting the robot's full dynamic capabilities.

Inspiration from biological systems, particularly legged animals' ability to visually perceive and adapt to their surroundings, has led to a significant shift towards deep reinforcement learning (RL) for synthesizing natural quadrupedal locomotion. Early RL approaches incorporated exteroceptive sensors like cameras and LiDAR to provide robots with environmental awareness. While these methods achieved impressive results in controlled environments, they faced inherent limitations in real-world scenarios. Exteroceptive sensors can be unreliable in adverse weather conditions, poor lighting, or when encountering visually ambiguous terrains (e.g., snow appearing solid but being soft, or tall grass appearing impassable).

To circumvent the unreliability of exteroceptive sensing, a separate line of research has explored using only proprioceptive sensors (e.g., IMUs, joint encoders), which are lighter and more robust. These proprioception-only methods attempt to infer terrain properties and body states implicitly. However, prior works in this domain have shown limited success in long-distance operations across highly challenging and varied terrains, often succumbing to accumulated estimation errors or uncertainties. A key challenge, often termed the "representation learning bottleneck," arises because the locomotion policy needs to understand terrain properties for robust behavior, but to learn these properties adequately, the robot must first be able to walk and explore diverse terrains. This creates a chicken-and-egg dilemma, hindering optimal policy learning.

DreamWaQ directly addresses this critical gap. It aims to develop a proprioception-only locomotion framework that overcomes the representation learning bottleneck, enabling robust and long-distance traversal of challenging, unstructured terrains. By implicitly inferring terrain properties and jointly learning a robust policy with an accurate environment representation, DreamWaQ positions itself as a significant advancement in enhancing the autonomy and reliability of quadrupedal robots for real-world deployment, especially in scenarios where exteroceptive sensing is compromised or unavailable. It builds upon and improves prior methods like AdaptationNet (which used a teacher-student paradigm for implicit environmental factor encoding) and EstimatorNet (which concurrently trained an estimator for body state), by proposing a more integrated and data-efficient architecture.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to propose a novel quadrupedal locomotion learning framework, named **DreamWaQ (Dream Walking for Quadrupedal Robots)**, that enables robots to walk robustly through challenging terrains using only limited sensing modalities, specifically proprioception.

The motivations behind this objective stem from the identified limitations of existing approaches:
- **Unreliability of Exteroceptive Sensors:** Current state-of-the-art methods that rely on cameras or LiDAR for perceiving terrain often fail in real-world adverse conditions (e.g., bad weather, poor lighting). Furthermore, these sensors can misinterpret terrain characteristics (e.g., snow appearing solid, tall grass appearing impassable). The authors are motivated to develop a system that does not depend on these fallible external sensors.
- **Limited Robustness of Proprioception-Only Methods:** While proprioceptive sensors (IMU, joint encoders) are robust and lightweight, previous works relying solely on them have demonstrated limited capabilities for sustained operation over long distances and varied, challenging terrains. They often struggle with high uncertainties and estimation errors, leading to failures.
- **Addressing the Representation Learning Bottleneck:** A fundamental challenge in proprioceptive locomotion is the circular dependency between learning robust locomotion and learning accurate terrain representations. The policy needs to understand the terrain to adapt, but it needs to walk well to explore and learn terrain properties. DreamWaQ is motivated to provide a learning framework that jointly learns a robust policy and an accurate environment representation, thereby circumventing this bottleneck.
- **Improving Data Efficiency:** The authors note that prior teacher-student training paradigms are data-inefficient and may bound the student policy's performance to the teacher's, limiting exploration of failure states. They aim for a more efficient, single-phase training approach.
- **Enhanced Real-World Practicality:** The motivation extends to practical real-world deployment, aiming for a policy that can sustain long-distance travel on diverse natural and man-made terrains, and specifically addressing issues like motor overheating by promoting balanced power distribution.

In summary, DreamWaQ seeks to achieve robust, adaptive, and long-duration quadrupedal locomotion in complex outdoor environments by inferring terrain properties implicitly from robust proprioceptive sensors, thereby overcoming the limitations of both exteroceptive and previous proprioception-only learning frameworks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DreamWaQ proposes a novel deep reinforcement learning framework centered on an **asymmetric actor-critic architecture** and a **Context-Aided Estimator Network (CENet)**. The overall system is trained entirely in simulation and deployed with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

**A. Asymmetric Actor-Critic Architecture:**
The core of DreamWaQ's learning mechanism is an asymmetric actor-critic setup, where the actor (policy network) and critic (value network) receive different levels of observation during training:
- **Policy Network (Actor):** This network, $\pi_\phi(a_t | o_t, v_t, z_t)$, outputs the desired joint angles ($a_t$). It receives **temporal partial observations ($o_t^H$)** from proprioceptive sensors (body angular velocity, gravity vector, body velocity command, joint angle, joint angular velocity, and previous action) *along with* the estimated body velocity ($v_t$) and a latent context vector ($z_t$) from CENet. The temporal observations (H=5) allow the policy to capture short-term dynamics. Crucially, the policy is trained to *implicitly infer* terrain properties (like height map, friction, obstacles) from these inputs.
- **Value Network (Critic):** This network, $V(s_t)$, estimates the state value. Unlike the policy, it receives **privileged observations ($s_t$)**, which include the full state information available in the simulator: proprioception, true body velocity, *disturbance forces*, and a *height map scan* of the surroundings. This "cheating" by the critic during training provides a rich signal for learning an optimal value function, which in turn guides the policy more effectively, even though the policy itself never sees these privileged observations.
- **Optimization:** The policy is optimized using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.

**B. Reward Function:**
The reward function is a weighted sum of various terms designed to encourage desired locomotion behavior. It includes:
- **Task Rewards:** For tracking commanded linear and angular velocities.
- **Stability Rewards:** Penalizing vertical linear velocity, lateral/roll angular velocity, and body orientation errors to ensure stable movement.
- **Motor/Joint Control Rewards:** Penalizing joint accelerations, power consumption, action rate, and encouraging smoothness.
- **Novel Power Distribution Reward:** A unique addition that penalizes high variance in motor power usage across all joints. This helps prevent specific motors from overheating in real-world scenarios by promoting a more balanced power distribution.

**C. Curriculum Learning:**
A game-inspired curriculum is used to progressively train the policy on increasingly difficult terrains, starting from smooth and progressing through rough, discretized, and stair terrains, with varying inclinations (up to 22°). A grid-adaptive curriculum is also utilized for low-speed locomotion to improve stable turning and prevent foot tripping.

**D. Context-Aided Estimator Network (CENet):**
This is a critical component that addresses the estimation challenge from proprioception.
- **Purpose:** CENet jointly estimates the robot's body linear velocity ($v_t$) and infers a latent representation of the environment's context ($z_t$) from temporal proprioceptive observations.
- **Architecture:** It employs a single encoder and a multi-head decoder architecture. The encoder processes temporal partial observations ($o_t^H$). One decoder head estimates $v_t$, while the second head reconstructs the next observation ($o_{t+1}$).
- **Learning Mechanism:** CENet is trained using a hybrid loss function:
 - **Estimation Loss ($L_{est}$):** Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the estimated body velocity and the ground truth from the simulator.
 - **VAE Loss ($L_{VAE}$):** This leverages a $\beta$-Variational Auto-Encoder ($\beta$-VAE). It consists of:
 - Reconstruction Loss (MSE): Between the reconstructed next observation and the true next observation, enabling the network to learn forward-backward dynamics.
 - Latent Loss (KL divergence): Encourages the latent context vector ($z_t$) to conform to a prior distribution (standard normal), promoting disentangled and meaningful representations of the environment.
- **Adaptive Bootstrapping (AdaBoot):** To mitigate the negative impact of noisy estimation early in training, DreamWaQ introduces AdaBoot. This method adaptively tunes the probability of bootstrapping from CENet's estimates (i.e., using CENet's output as input to the policy) based on the Coefficient of Variation (CV) of episodic rewards across multiple domain-randomized environments. When the CV is low (indicating stable learning), bootstrapping probability is high; when CV is high (indicating unstable learning), bootstrapping is reduced. This ensures robust policy learning by leveraging accurate estimates when available, but not relying on unreliable ones.

**E. Simulation and Training:**
Training is performed using the Isaac Gym simulator, which allows for massively parallel training (4096 agents concurrently). Extensive domain randomization (varying payload, motor gains, center of mass shift, friction, system delay) is applied to enhance sim-to-real transferability and policy robustness. The training process takes approximately one hour, generating data equivalent to 46 days of real-world experience.

This comprehensive methodology, combining a sophisticated actor-critic setup with a jointly trained context-aware estimator and adaptive bootstrapping, aims to enable the robot to achieve robust locomotion by implicitly "imagining" the terrain, even with limited sensing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating DreamWaQ's superior performance and robustness.

**A. Simulation Performance (Learning Curves):**
- DreamWaQ consistently achieved higher mean episodic rewards compared to baseline methods (Baseline, AdaptationNet, EstimatorNet) throughout the training process.
- Despite operating solely on proprioception, DreamWaQ's performance closely matched that of an "oracle" policy, which had direct access to the terrain height map. This is a significant finding, indicating its ability to implicitly infer crucial environmental information.
- EstimatorNet initially showed promise but its performance declined in more challenging terrains, highlighting the limitations of explicit state estimation without environmental context.

**B. Command Tracking Performance (Gazebo Simulation):**
- DreamWaQ significantly reduced the Absolute Tracking Error (ATE) for linear velocities (forward and lateral) and angular (yaw) velocity compared to all other methods.
- Statistical analysis (paired t-test, p-value < 10^-4) confirmed the superiority of DreamWaQ.
- The inclusion of **AdaBoot** further improved DreamWaQ's command tracking performance, demonstrating its effectiveness in enhancing robustness through adaptive bootstrapping.

**C. Explicit Estimation Comparison (CENet vs. EstimatorNet):**
- In normal walking conditions on flat terrain, CENet showed small estimation errors, attributed to its ability to learn forward-backward dynamics via the VAE's auto-encoding mechanism.
- CENet's advantage became particularly evident when the robot encountered challenging scenarios, such as stumbling down stairs. While EstimatorNet exhibited significant errors in body velocity estimation during these events, CENet maintained high accuracy. This suggests that CENet's joint learning of body state and environmental context allows it to implicitly "reason" about the terrain, leading to more robust and accurate state estimation, which is critical for preventing catastrophic failures.

**D. Robustness Analysis (Simulation):**
- DreamWaQ (especially with AdaBoot) demonstrated exceptional robustness to external disturbances. It could withstand significantly higher maximum push velocities (1.121 ± 0.164 m/s with AdaBoot) compared to other methods (e.g., Baseline at 0.511 m/s, AdaptationNet at 0.714 m/s).
- The survival rate (percentage of time the robot survived a random walk over 30 minutes) was also substantially higher for DreamWaQ with AdaBoot (95.23%), far exceeding the baseline (20.51%) and outperforming other adaptive methods. This quantitative evidence supports the claim of increased robustness through accurate estimation and robust policy learning.

**E. Real-World Demonstration (Unitree A1 Robot):**
- **Foot Reflex and Adaptation:** The robot exhibited adaptive gaits and immediate foot reflex actions when encountering uncertainties like stumbling and slipping. It could adapt its body posture and foot placement for going downstairs (tilting body, extending front foot) and upstairs (increasing foot steps). It also demonstrated quick recovery from irregular footholds.
- **Long-Distance Walk:** A critical validation was performed on a Unitree A1 robot across two challenging outdoor courses at KAIST:
 - **Course A (430m):** An unstructured campus yard with various slopes, deformable terrains (mud), thick vegetation, and stairs. DreamWaQ enabled the robot to successfully navigate these challenging conditions, including wet and slippery stairs and muddy slopes, without difficulties.
 - **Course B (465m):** A hiking track on a moderately high hill with an elevation gain of 22m, featuring asphalt, gravel, and slopes. Despite the small size of the Unitree A1 and potential motor overheating during climbs, DreamWaQ allowed the robot to complete the entire trajectory within 10 minutes and reach the summit.
- The success on a smaller robot like the Unitree A1, often not demonstrated for such challenging long-distance traversals, further emphasizes the policy's robustness and generalizability.

Overall, the findings strongly support DreamWaQ's ability to learn highly robust and adaptive quadrupedal locomotion policies using only proprioception, achieving near-oracle performance in simulation and proving its practical efficacy in diverse, challenging real-world outdoor environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DreamWaQ framework represents a significant advancement in the field of quadrupedal robot locomotion, with profound implications for the development and deployment of autonomous legged robots in unstructured environments.

**1. Enhanced Robustness and Reliability with Limited Sensing:** The most significant contribution is the demonstration of highly robust quadrupedal locomotion relying solely on proprioceptive sensors. This bypasses the fragility and limitations of exteroceptive sensors (cameras, LiDAR) in adverse conditions, making robots more reliable for real-world applications such as inspection in industrial sites, disaster relief, and exploration in harsh weather or low-light scenarios. The ability to implicitly "imagine" terrain properties from internal cues is a crucial step towards truly autonomous and resilient legged robots.

**2. Overcoming the Representation Learning Bottleneck:** DreamWaQ effectively solves the long-standing "representation learning bottleneck" by jointly training a locomotion policy with an accurate environment representation via the Context-Aided Estimator Network (CENet). This integrated approach allows the policy to adapt to unknown terrains without explicit external terrain maps, marking a conceptual leap in how legged robots perceive and interact with their environment.

**3. Data and Training Efficiency:** The adoption of an asymmetric actor-critic architecture and a single-phase training pipeline significantly improves data efficiency compared to traditional teacher-student paradigms. This translates to faster training times (one hour for 46 days of real-world equivalent data), which is critical for rapid iteration and deployment in research and industry.

**4. Advanced State Estimation:** The proposed CENet, with its shared encoder and auto-encoding mechanism (Beta-VAE), provides more accurate and robust body velocity and context estimation, especially during challenging events like stumbling. This improved internal state representation is foundational for agile and stable locomotion in dynamic and uncertain environments.

**5. Adaptive Bootstrapping for Improved Generalization:** The novel AdaBoot method intelligently leverages estimator outputs during training, preventing performance degradation from early-stage noisy estimates while ensuring the policy benefits from more accurate estimations as training progresses. This contributes to better generalization and real-world transferability.

**6. Practical Demonstrations on a Smaller Robot:** Successfully demonstrating long-distance, stable locomotion on challenging outdoor terrains (slopes, stairs, mud, gravel, thick vegetation) with a Unitree A1 robot is particularly impactful. The Unitree A1 is a smaller and lighter robot compared to larger platforms like ANYmal, making its robust performance a testament to the efficiency and effectiveness of the DreamWaQ policy, and highlighting its potential for broader adoption in various applications where smaller, more agile robots are required.

**7. Contribution to Real-World Deployability:** By addressing issues like motor overheating through the power distribution reward and demonstrating robust performance in varied natural terrains, the work moves beyond theoretical advancements towards truly deployable robotic solutions.

In conclusion, DreamWaQ sets a new benchmark for proprioception-only locomotion for quadrupedal robots. Its significance lies in its innovative methodological contributions and, more importantly, its compelling empirical validation in real-world, long-distance outdoor scenarios, paving the way for more robust, autonomous, and widely applicable legged robots. Future work, as acknowledged by the authors, may involve integrating exteroception for proactive gait planning in highly complex, high-rise obstacle environments, but DreamWaQ's current capabilities are a substantial step forward.
