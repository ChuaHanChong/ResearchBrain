# A comprehensive taxonomy of hallucinations in Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A detailed taxonomy of hallucinations in Large Language Models is presented, formally defining them as an inherent, inevitable characteristic of computable models and categorizing their various types, underlying causes, evaluation methods, and mitigation strategies, alongside considerations for human factors and monitoring.

## Problem

- Large Language Models frequently generate plausible yet factually incorrect or fabricated content, hindering their reliable deployment.
- The research landscape lacks a unified and comprehensive taxonomy for categorizing different types of LLM hallucinations.
- Inconsistent definitions and categorizations across literature impede comparative research and the development of standardized mitigation strategies.

## Method

- A ==systematic review and taxonomic analysis== approach is used to consolidate and structure existing knowledge on LLM hallucinations.
- The work establishes a ==formal definition of hallucination== and incorporates a theoretical framework positing its ==inherent inevitability== in computable LLMs.
- A ==multi-layered taxonomy== categorizes hallucinations based on ==core distinctions==, ==specific manifestations==, ==underlying causes==, and integrates ==human factors==.

## Results

- A comprehensive multi-layered taxonomy details core distinctions such as intrinsic vs. extrinsic and factuality vs. faithfulness hallucinations, along with specific manifestations.
- Underlying causes are categorized into data-related (e.g., quality, bias), model-related (e.g., auto-regressive nature, training issues), and prompt-related factors.
- Existing evaluation benchmarks and mitigation strategies, including RAG and guardrails, are surveyed, with a recommendation for hybrid, context-aware mitigation systems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hallucination is formally defined as an inconsistency with computable ground truth and is an inherently inevitable characteristic of computable LLMs.
> - The inherent nature of hallucination mandates a shift in research focus from elimination to robust detection, management, and continuous human oversight.
> - Human cognitive biases and over-reliance on LLMs significantly influence the impact and perception of hallucinations, highlighting the need for user-centric design.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models have undeniably revolutionized Natural Language Processing (NLP), enabling unprecedented capabilities in content generation, question answering, and conversational AI. However, their pervasive "hallucination"—the generation of plausible yet factually incorrect or fabricated content—remains a formidable obstacle to their reliable and responsible deployment. The broader research landscape is currently grappling with how to effectively define, detect, and mitigate these hallucinations.

Cossio's work directly addresses a significant gap in this landscape: the lack of a unified, comprehensive, and widely accepted taxonomy for LLM hallucinations. Existing literature often employs inconsistent definitions and categorizations, hindering comparative research and the development of generalized solutions. This report consolidates and systematizes the diverse understandings of hallucination, providing a foundational framework for the field.

Furthermore, a pivotal contribution of this work is its explicit embrace of the theoretical inevitability of hallucination in computable LLMs, drawing heavily from the formal framework proposed by Xu et al. (2024). This paradigm shift—from viewing hallucination as a remediable bug to an inherent limitation—is crucial. It redirects research efforts from aiming for complete eradication to focusing on robust detection, effective mitigation strategies, and continuous human oversight. By synthesizing knowledge on types, causes, evaluation methodologies, and mitigation techniques, this report serves as a critical meta-analysis, offering a holistic perspective necessary for practitioners and researchers navigating the complexities of trustworthy AI. It also explicitly integrates human factors and cognitive biases, bridging technical AI challenges with human-computer interaction and responsible AI development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this report is to establish a detailed and comprehensive taxonomy of hallucinations in Large Language Models. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing a Critical Challenge:** The propensity of LLMs to generate plausible but incorrect content is a major limitation undermining their trustworthiness and reliability, especially as they are increasingly integrated into critical applications like information retrieval and decision support systems.
2. **Lack of Unified Understanding:** The existing scientific literature suffers from inconsistent definitions, categorizations, and an absence of a unified framework for understanding LLM hallucinations. This fragmentation hinders effective research, benchmarking, and the development of standardized mitigation strategies.
3. **Formalizing the Problem:** The report seeks to provide a formal definition of hallucination and present a theoretical framework (based on Xu et al., 2024) that posits its inherent inevitability in computable LLMs. This formal grounding is motivated by the need to fundamentally redefine the problem statement, moving from an expectation of complete elimination to a focus on robust management.
4. **Synthesizing Disparate Knowledge:** By exploring the core distinctions, detailing specific manifestations, analyzing underlying causes (data, model, prompt-related), and surveying evaluation and mitigation techniques, the report aims to consolidate scattered knowledge into a coherent, comprehensive resource.
5. **Guiding Responsible Deployment:** Ultimately, the motivation is to foster the responsible and reliable deployment of LLMs, particularly in critical applications, by emphasizing the need for robust detection, mitigation, and continuous human oversight, given the complex, multifaceted, and theoretically inevitable nature of hallucinations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This report employs a **systematic review and taxonomic analysis** approach rather than empirical experimentation. The methodology is characterized by its comprehensive scope and structured organization of existing knowledge:

1. **Literature Review and Synthesis:** The core of the methodology involves an extensive review of the scientific literature pertaining to LLM hallucinations. The author draws upon numerous scholarly articles, conference papers, and preprints to gather definitions, examples, underlying causes, evaluation methods, and mitigation strategies.
2. **Formal Definition and Theoretical Foundation:** The report begins by establishing a formal conceptual and mathematical definition of hallucination within LLMs. It rigorously incorporates the theoretical framework from Xu et al. (2024), which uses diagonalization arguments from computability theory to posit the inherent inevitability of hallucination in any computable LLM. This theoretical underpinning serves as a foundational premise for the entire taxonomy and subsequent discussions.
3. **Hierarchical Categorization:** The author systematically organizes hallucinations into a multi-layered taxonomy:
 - **Core Distinctions:** Primary categorizations are established (e.g., intrinsic vs. extrinsic, factuality vs. faithfulness).
 - **Specific Manifestations:** These core distinctions are then broken down into granular types of hallucinations (e.g., factual errors, logical inconsistencies, ethical violations, task-specific errors across domains like code generation and multimodal applications).
4. **Causal Analysis:** The report meticulously categorizes the underlying causes of hallucinations into three main groups: data-related factors, model-related factors (architecture, training, decoding), and prompt-related influences.
5. **Human Factors Integration:** Uniquely, the methodology extends beyond technical aspects to integrate cognitive and human factors, analyzing how user trust, interpretability, and cognitive biases influence the perception and impact of hallucinations. This involves reviewing psychological and human-computer interaction literature.
6. **Evaluation and Mitigation Survey:** The report systematically surveys existing evaluation benchmarks and quantitative metrics used for hallucination detection, critically assessing their strengths and limitations. Concurrently, it reviews a broad spectrum of architectural and systemic mitigation strategies proposed in the literature.
7. **Resource Identification:** The methodology culminates in identifying and describing key web-based resources and dashboards that track LLM releases, performance, and hallucination trends, providing practical tools for ongoing monitoring.

By structuring the analysis from formal definitions to practical monitoring tools, the report offers a holistic and systematic understanding of the hallucination problem, grounded in theoretical computer science and extended through interdisciplinary insights.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The report presents a comprehensive set of findings, structured as a multi-layered taxonomy of LLM hallucinations:

- **Formal Definition and Inevitability:** Hallucination is formally defined as an inconsistency between a computable LLM's output and a computable ground truth function. A central finding, drawing from Xu et al. (2024), is that hallucination is an *inherently inevitable* characteristic of computable LLMs, regardless of architectural design, training methods, or data. This is proven through diagonalization arguments, implying that complete elimination is impossible and efforts must shift to detection and management.
- **Core Taxonomies:**
 - **Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Hallucinations:** Intrinsic hallucinations contradict the provided input context (e.g., a summary misrepresenting source text). Extrinsic hallucinations are inconsistent with training data or reality, introducing information not supported by or refutable by the input (e.g., fabricating a non-existent historical event).
 - **Factuality vs. Faithfulness Hallucinations:** Factuality hallucinations involve content that is absolutely incorrect compared to real-world knowledge (e.g., "The Great Wall of China is visible from space"). Faithfulness hallucinations occur when the output diverges from the input prompt or provided context, even if internally consistent (e.g., summary contradicting the source article).
- **Specific Manifestations:**
 - **Factual Errors & Fabrications:** Includes incorrect facts (e.g., Bard's exoplanet image claim), fabricated entities/information (e.g., fake court cases in legal contexts, made-up disease details), and adversarial attacks exploiting prompt-embedded falsehoods.
 - **Contextual & Instruction Inconsistencies:** Outputs that contradict or diverge from the provided context or fail to follow explicit user instructions (e.g., translating a question but answering in English).
 - **Logical & Temporal Inconsistencies:** Internal logical errors, self-contradictory statements, and issues with time-sensitive information, leading to outdated or anachronistic facts (e.g., incorrect Nobel Prize assertions).
 - **Ethical Violations:** Hallucinations with severe real-world consequences, such as defamation, financial misinformation (e.g., incorrect refund info from a chatbot), and legal inaccuracies (e.g., bogus judicial decisions).
 - **Amalgamated & Nonsensical Responses:** Incorrectly combining multiple facts from a prompt or generating completely irrelevant and illogical output.
 - **Task-Specific Hallucinations:** Distinct manifestations across applications, including dialogue history mix-ups, abstractive summarization distortions, erroneous inferences in QA, incorrect code generation (e.g., syntactic/semantic errors), and cross-modal inconsistencies in Multimodal LLMs (e.g., identifying nonexistent objects in images).
- **Underlying Causes:** Hallucinations arise from a complex interplay of:
 - **Data-related factors:** Low quality/volume, inadequate representation/biases, outdated information, and source-reference divergence in training data.
 - **Model-related factors:** The auto-regressive nature (token prediction over factual accuracy), architectural flaws, training issues (exposure/capability/belief misalignment, over-optimization), stochastic decoding strategies, overconfidence/poor calibration, generalization failures, lack of true reasoning, knowledge overshadowing, and insufficient knowledge representation.
 - **Prompt-related factors:** Adversarial attacks and the model's overly confirmatory tendency, along with poorly structured prompts.
- **Cognitive and Human Factors:** Users often over-rely on LLMs due to fluency heuristics and cognitive biases (automation bias, confirmation bias, illusion of explanatory depth), often failing to notice hallucinations even with explicit warnings. This necessitates user-centered design, including calibrated uncertainty displays, source-grounding indicators, justification prompts, and factuality-aware interfaces.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks and Metrics:** The report surveys prominent benchmarks (TruthfulQA, HalluLens, FActScore, Q2/QuestEval, MedHallu, CodeHaluEval, HALLUCINOGEN) and quantitative metrics (ROUGE, BLEU, BERTScore for faithfulness; FactCC, SummaC for consistency; KILT, RAE for factuality), alongside the indispensable role of human evaluation. It highlights limitations such as lack of standardization, task dependence, insensitivity to subtle errors, and limited explainability.
- **Mitigation Strategies:**
 - **Architectural:** Toolformer-style augmentation (using external APIs), Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for factual grounding, and fine-tuning with synthetic or adversarially filtered data.
 - **Systemic:** Guardrails (logic validators, factual filters, rule-based fallbacks) and symbolic integration to constrain model behavior. The report advocates for **hybrid and context-aware mitigation systems** combining multiple strategies tailored to specific application domains.
- **Monitoring Resources:** The report introduces practical web-based tools like Artificial Analysis, Vectara Hallucination Leaderboard, Epoch AI Benchmarking Dashboard, and LM Arena, which provide real-time performance tracking, intelligence indices, cost analysis, and user preference data for various LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This comprehensive taxonomy and analysis by Manuel Cossio carries significant implications for the future development, evaluation, and deployment of Large Language Models.

Firstly, the report's central finding regarding the **inherent inevitability of hallucinations** in computable LLMs represents a crucial theoretical contribution. It necessitates a fundamental shift in the research paradigm, moving away from the elusive goal of complete eradication towards designing robust systems that can effectively detect, mitigate, and manage hallucinated content. This understanding is vital for setting realistic expectations for AI capabilities and fostering more pragmatic research directions.

Secondly, by providing a **structured and detailed taxonomy**, the report offers a much-needed common language and framework for researchers and practitioners. This standardization can significantly improve the clarity, comparability, and cumulative progress of research into hallucination. It enables more precise identification of error types, allowing for the development of targeted detection and mitigation strategies rather than generic, often ineffective, solutions.

Thirdly, the in-depth analysis of **underlying causes** (data, model, and prompt-related) provides actionable insights for improving LLM design and training. Understanding that factors like auto-regressive nature, training biases, or poor decoding strategies contribute to hallucinations can guide developers in building more robust and reliable models, for instance, by emphasizing better data curation, architectural enhancements, or advanced decoding methods.

Fourthly, the integration of **cognitive and human factors** is particularly impactful. By demonstrating how user trust and biases can amplify the risks associated with hallucinations, the report underscores the necessity of user-centric design. This emphasizes that technical solutions alone are insufficient; effective interfaces must incorporate elements like uncertainty displays, source-grounding, and justification prompts to empower users to critically evaluate AI outputs, particularly in high-stakes environments.

Finally, the survey of **evaluation benchmarks, mitigation strategies, and monitoring resources** offers immediate practical value. It serves as a go-to reference for researchers and industry practitioners looking to assess LLM performance, implement effective safeguards, and stay abreast of the rapidly evolving landscape. The emphasis on **hybrid and context-aware mitigation systems** highlights a pragmatic path forward, suggesting that combining multiple strategies tailored to specific application domains will be the most effective approach.

In essence, this report is a foundational document for the responsible development of LLMs. It underscores that while the transformative potential of LLMs is immense, their safe and trustworthy integration into society hinges upon a profound understanding of their inherent limitations and a concerted effort to manage, rather than eliminate, the challenge of hallucination through interdisciplinary research and continuous human oversight. This is particularly critical for applications in medicine, law, and finance, where factual accuracy is not merely a performance metric but a prerequisite for ethical and safe operation.
