# Mitigate the Gap: Investigating Approaches for Improving Cross-Modal Alignment in CLIP

> [!summary] Summary
> AlignCLIP improves the cross-modal alignment in CLIP by addressing the modality gap, where image and text embeddings for the same concept remain distant. It achieves better alignment through shared encoder parameters and a novel semantically-regularized intra-modality separation loss, leading to enhanced performance across zero-shot classification, robustness benchmarks, and multi-modal retrieval tasks.

## Problem

- The Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP) model exhibits a "modality gap," where image and text embeddings for the same semantic concept do not perfectly align, populating distinct sub-regions of the embedding space.
- This modality gap results in a sparse and disconnected embedding space, hindering effective cross-modal understanding and potentially limiting downstream task performance.
- Existing solutions often involve altering training data or using naive isomorphic translations that may distort semantic structure, rather than intrinsically improving the representation space.

## Method

- Introduced ==SharedCLIP==, an architectural modification where the transformer encoder and projection layer are shared between vision and language modalities to compel a more unified mapping function.
- Developed an ==Intra-Modality Separation (IMSep) objective==, a vision-to-vision contrastive loss, to explicitly regularize distances within the image modality.
- Integrated ==semantic regularization== into IMSep using a pre-trained sentence encoder to prevent the erroneous separation of semantically similar images, thus preserving the embedding space's integrity.

## Results

- AlignCLIP achieved the best cross-modal alignment scores (**0.62-0.67**), reducing the average angle between paired multi-modal samples to **47 degrees**, a substantial improvement over CLIP's **61-68 degrees**.
- Consistently outperformed original CLIP in zero-shot image classification, showing up to **8%** (Top-1) improvement on CIFAR-10 and about **8%** (Top-1) on CIFAR-100, and also improved performance with linear probing.
- Demonstrated enhanced robustness to natural distribution shifts, achieving **1-2%** improvements in Top1 and Top5 accuracy across ImageNetV2, ImageNet-R, ImageNet-A, and ImageNetSketch datasets compared to CLIP.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sharing parameters between vision and language encoders (SharedCLIP) significantly reduces the modality gap, suggesting that a unified architectural backbone naturally fosters better multi-modal alignment.
> - Explicitly enforcing intra-modality separation for image embeddings, combined with semantic regularization (AlignCLIP's IMSep), is crucial for creating a more coherent and globally meaningful embedding space.
> - The cross-modal objective provides sufficient supervision for text embeddings, as explicitly enforcing intra-modality separation on text embeddings can be detrimental to performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper is situated within the rapidly evolving landscape of multi-modal machine learning, specifically focusing on **Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP)**. OpenAI's CLIP model (Radford et al., 2021) has become a cornerstone in vision-language research due to its exceptional zero-shot classification capabilities, robustness to natural distribution shifts, and utility in various cross-modal tasks like retrieval. CLIP achieves this by learning a shared embedding space where images and text can be jointly represented, and positive (paired) samples are brought closer while negative samples are pushed apart.

Despite CLIP's success, recent research has identified a significant shortcoming: the **modality gap**. This refers to the phenomenon where, even after training, image and text embeddings for the same semantic concept do not perfectly align. Instead, each modality tends to densely populate distinct sub-regions of the shared embedding hypersphere, making the space sparse and disconnected. Previous works, such as Liang et al. (2022) and Tyshchuk et al. (2023), have attributed this gap to factors like model initialization and the nature of the contrastive loss. Schrodi et al. (2024) further linked the modality gap to information imbalance between modalities, where text descriptions are often less detailed than their corresponding images. Jiang et al. (2023) explored the information gap and proposed regularization methods using augmented samples.

This work distinguishes itself from prior efforts by specifically investigating architectural and loss-based modifications to *mitigate the modality gap under the same amount of information imbalance* (i.e., without altering the training data characteristics or balance). Unlike some previous approaches that might involve naive isomorphic translations which could inadvertently distort the semantic structure of the embedding space for unpaired samples, this research aims for modifications that intrinsically improve the semantic structure. Furthermore, while other studies have proposed various modifications to CLIP (e.g., UniCLIP, SLIP, CyCLIP, xCLIP, SigLIP, ReCLIP) to improve specific aspects like robustness or efficiency, this paper’s core focus is the *direct investigation of cross-modal alignment and the modality gap* as a primary objective, alongside its impact on downstream performance. By tackling this fundamental geometric issue, the authors aim to enhance the foundational quality of CLIP's representation space.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to address and mitigate the pronounced modality gap observed in the CLIP embedding space. The authors argue that this gap hinders effective cross-modal alignment, leading to a sparse and disconnected embedding space where image and text representations of the same concept remain significantly distant. The overarching goal is to reduce this gap and, consequently, improve the performance of CLIP across various downstream tasks, all while operating under the inherent information imbalance of existing datasets.

To achieve this, the paper sets out to answer three specific research questions:

1. **Does sharing the parameter space between the multi-modal encoders reduce the modality gap?** This question explores an architectural modification, hypothesizing that having separate, disentangled parameters for vision and language encoders might contribute to their divergence in the embedding space.
2. **Can the gap be further mitigated by pushing apart the uni-modal embeddings via intra-modality separation?** This question investigates a loss-based approach, proposing to explicitly regularize the distances *within* each modality to encourage a more spread-out and better-aligned distribution.
3. **How do these gap reduction approaches affect the downstream performance?** This crucial question assesses the practical implications of their proposed modifications, ensuring that improvements in cross-modal alignment do not come at the expense of, but rather enhance, performance in real-world applications.

By systematically addressing these questions, the authors aim to develop a refined CLIP model, named AlignCLIP, that achieves superior cross-modal alignment without sacrificing, and ideally improving, its impressive downstream capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, **AlignCLIP**, introduces two main refinements to the standard CLIP architecture and training objective: parameter sharing between encoders and a novel intra-modality separation loss.

#### 3.1. Sharing the Learnable Parameter Space (SharedCLIP)

To investigate the first research question, the authors propose **SharedCLIP**, an architecture where the transformer encoder and the subsequent projection layer are shared between the vision and language modalities. The intuition is that separate parameter sets for vision and language encoders in original CLIP allow them to diverge significantly during optimization, leading to distinct sub-regions for each modality in the embedding space. By sharing these critical components, the model is compelled to learn a more unified and aligned mapping function for both modalities. The architecture uses a standard transformer encoder, with specific input processing for each modality (e.g., patch embeddings for images, token embeddings for text), followed by the shared transformer layers and a shared projection head. Max-pooling is used for text embeddings and the [CLS] token embedding for image embeddings, consistent with common practices.

#### 3.2. Intra-Modality Separation (IMSep)

Addressing the second research question, AlignCLIP introduces an **Intra-Modality Separation (IMSep)** objective. The hypothesis here is that CLIP's cross-modal contrastive loss primarily focuses on aligning paired image-text samples while pushing apart *unpaired* cross-modal samples. However, it does not explicitly impose meaningful distance regularization *within* a single modality (e.g., between different images or different texts). This lack of intra-modality structure can contribute to the dense clustering of same-modality embeddings in isolated sub-regions.

IMSep is implemented as a vision-to-vision contrastive loss ($L_{v \to v}$). In this loss, the image-text pairs still serve as positive samples, but now, any pairwise combination of *image-image* within a batch is considered a negative sample. The critical innovation lies in **semantic regularization** of this intra-modality separation. A naive application of $L_{v \to v}$ could erroneously push apart semantically similar images. To prevent this, the authors leverage a pre-trained sentence encoder (SBERT all-mpnet-base-v2) to derive semantic similarity scores for the *text captions* corresponding to the images in the batch. These text-based semantic similarities (S) are converted into distances (D = 1 - S) and then used to re-scale the pairwise cosine similarities of the *image embeddings* (V). Specifically, the image-image similarity matrix `V` is element-wise multiplied by `D` (`V_D = V ⊙ D`). This re-scaling ensures that if two images are semantically similar (i.e., their corresponding texts are similar, leading to a small `D` value), their dot product in `V_D` is reduced. Consequently, the intra-modality loss does not strongly minimize the dot products of these semantically similar image pairs, allowing them to remain closer.

The final Intra-Modality Separation loss ($L_{IMsep}$) is then calculated using these semantically regularized vision-vision logits alongside the positive image-text logits. The overall loss function for AlignCLIP combines the standard CLIP cross-modal separation loss ($L_{CRsep}$) and the proposed $L_{IMsep}$: $L = \alpha L_{CRsep} + \beta L_{IMsep}$, where $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=0.5$ were chosen. The authors demonstrate that applying IMSep solely to image embeddings is sufficient, as the cross-modal objective implicitly regularizes the text embeddings.

#### 3.3. Training Setup

The models were pre-trained from scratch on the Conceptual Caption 12M (CC12M) dataset, a large-scale public dataset, using the OpenCLIP implementation. A ViT-B-16 backend was used for fair comparison with original CLIP. Training involved 30 epochs with a batch size of 512 on an NVIDIA H100 GPU, utilizing AdamW optimization with a cosine scheduler and specific learning rate, warmup steps, and weight decay parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experiments conducted by the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of AlignCLIP in mitigating the modality gap while enhancing performance across various downstream tasks.

#### 4.1. Cross-Modal Alignment Score

- **Original CLIP:** Exhibited low alignment scores (0.38 to 0.47), indicating an average angle between paired image-text embeddings of 61 to 68 degrees, confirming the pronounced modality gap.
- **SharedCLIP:** Showed noticeable improvements, increasing alignment scores by up to 0.17 points, reducing the average angle to approximately 51 degrees.
- **AlignCLIP:** Achieved the best alignment scores, ranging from 0.62 to 0.67, corresponding to an average angle of 47 degrees between paired multi-modal samples.
- **Visualization:** DOSNES projections (Figure 5) qualitatively supported these findings, showing that CLIP's embeddings cluster in distinct sub-regions, SharedCLIP spreads them out more, and AlignCLIP achieves the best spread and alignment, reducing sparsity. Cumulative distribution plots (Figure 4) further confirmed that AlignCLIP shifts the distribution of positive sample cosine similarities significantly towards higher values.

#### 4.2. Classification Performance

- **Zero-Shot Image Classification:**
 - SharedCLIP showed mixed results, slightly reducing accuracy on some datasets (CIFAR-10/100, Flowers-102, Stanford Cars) but slightly improving on ImageNet-1K.
 - AlignCLIP consistently yielded the best performance across all five datasets (ImageNet-1K, CIFAR-100, CIFAR-10, Flowers-102, Stanford Cars). For instance, it achieved up to 8% (Top-1) and 2% (Top-5) improvements on CIFAR-10 and about 8% (Top-1) and 11% (Top-5) on CIFAR-100 compared to original CLIP.
- **Image Classification with Linear Probing:**
 - AlignCLIP demonstrated superiority across all five datasets when a linear classifier was trained on top of its frozen embeddings, further validating the quality of its learned representations.

#### 4.3. Robustness to Natural Distribution Shift

- Evaluations on ImageNetV2, ImageNet-R, ImageNet-A, and ImageNetSketch datasets revealed that SharedCLIP generally improved classification accuracy over CLIP.
- AlignCLIP consistently achieved the best classification results across all these out-of-distribution datasets, showing about 1-2% improvements in Top1 and Top5 accuracy compared to CLIP, indicating enhanced generalizability.

#### 4.4. Multi-Modal Retrieval

- **Zero-Shot Transfer:** Both SharedCLIP and AlignCLIP improved zero-shot image-to-text and text-to-image retrieval results (R@{1, 5, 10}) on MSCOCO and Flickr30K datasets compared to CLIP. AlignCLIP generally achieved the best overall results, with SharedCLIP outperforming it in a few specific R@10 metrics.
- **Fine-tuned Retrieval:** When fine-tuned on MSCOCO and Flickr, both SharedCLIP and AlignCLIP outperformed CLIP. AlignCLIP generally maintained a slight edge over SharedCLIP, demonstrating its robust performance even after domain-specific fine-tuning.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study on Re-scaling Mechanism

The ablation study confirmed the critical role of the semantic re-scaling mechanism within the IMSep loss. AlignCLIP with the re-scaling mechanism consistently outperformed AlignCLIP without it across various tasks, underscoring its effectiveness in judiciously controlling the separation of similar image samples and maintaining the semantic integrity of the embedding space.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Examples

Qualitative analysis on MSCOCO samples illustrated that AlignCLIP successfully identified the semantically more precise ground truth captions for images, even when other captions conveyed similar general semantics. This was particularly evident where CLIP and SharedCLIP might favor less precise captions, suggesting that the semantic regularization of IMSep contributes to a more nuanced understanding of intra-modal relationships.

#### 4.7. Effects of Text Embedding Separation (Appendix A.2)

An additional study showed that explicitly enforcing intra-modality separation on text embeddings (AlignCLIP-TT) or both modalities (AlignCLIP-II-TT) generally led to *worse* performance in zero-shot classification and linear probing compared to only separating image embeddings (AlignCLIP-II, the main proposed model). This supports the authors' claim that the cross-modal objective provides sufficient supervision for text embeddings, rendering explicit text-only separation redundant or even detrimental.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the field of multi-modal representation learning, particularly in understanding and improving foundational models like CLIP.

The primary significance lies in the **demonstration that the pronounced modality gap in CLIP can be substantially mitigated without sacrificing, and indeed while improving, downstream task performance.** This is a crucial finding, as efforts to correct fundamental representational issues often come with trade-offs. AlignCLIP achieves this by proposing elegant modifications that refine the semantic structure of the latent space rather than merely adjusting feature vectors post-hoc.

Specifically, the work highlights:
1. **The effectiveness of parameter sharing** between vision and language encoders, suggesting that a unified architectural backbone can naturally lead to more aligned multi-modal representations.
2. **The power of semantically-regularized intra-modality separation**, a novel approach that addresses the lack of explicit internal structure within modalities in original CLIP. By ensuring that semantically similar samples within a modality are not unduly separated, it fosters a more coherent and globally meaningful embedding space.
3. **Comprehensive performance gains:** AlignCLIP consistently improves zero-shot classification, linear probing, robustness to distribution shifts, and multi-modal retrieval tasks, indicating that a better-aligned embedding space translates directly into a more powerful and versatile model.

The potential impact of this work is multi-faceted:
- **Improved Foundation Models:** The proposed techniques can lead to more robust, generalizable, and semantically consistent pre-trained vision-language models. These improvements can propagate to a wide array of downstream applications that leverage CLIP embeddings.
- **Enhanced Practical Applications:** Better cross-modal alignment means more accurate retrieval, more reliable zero-shot classification, and greater robustness in real-world scenarios, particularly where out-of-distribution data is common.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** This study provides a clear direction for future research in multi-modal learning, emphasizing the importance of shared architectures and sophisticated intra-modality regularization. It encourages further exploration of how to enforce semantic consistency within and across modalities more effectively.

**Limitations** acknowledged by the authors include the scope of classification benchmarks tested, the study being limited to the English language, and the non-exhaustive benchmarking of the pre-trained sentence encoder used for re-scaling. These suggest avenues for future extensions of this work.

The **Impact Statement** also thoughtfully addresses potential negative societal implications, such as the propagation of harmful biases and stereotypes present in web-crawled training data, and the potential for misuse in generating trademarked characters or notable figures. This highlights a responsible approach to AI research, urging careful consideration before deploying such powerful models in real-world applications.
