# RouteRLT: Learning When and Which RL Specialist Should Control a Vision-Language-Action Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> RouteRLT is a generalist-specialist routing framework for robot manipulation. A frozen vision-language-action policy handles most of a task, while lightweight RL specialists take over precision-critical phases. A phase selector reads the VLA's own compact latent to predict controller ownership, a hysteresis stabilizer suppresses transient switches, and an action-boundary manager invalidates stale chunk suffixes. Full-task success rises from 85.00% to 92.22% in LIBERO and insertion success from 6.7% to 35.0% on hardware.

## Problem

- Behavior-cloned VLA policies cannot exceed demonstration precision, so they stall or misalign during contact-rich stages like grasp closure and connector seating.
- Prior RL refinement fixes the refinement scope with signals absent at deployment: an operator choosing the handoff point, or a privileged router reading unobservable simulator state.
- No prior system predicts ownership over a bank of RL specialists from the generalist's own internal state, at every control query, and honors it mid-chunk.

## Method

- **Latent-space phase router**: a causal two-latent window from the frozen VLA's appended readout token feeds a shared linear projector, trained with ==class-balanced cross-entropy== to predict the active controller.
- Three-phase ==stabilizer state machine== with asymmetric enter/exit thresholds and dwell counters plus an exit-only occupancy minimum, grid-searched on validation under entry-recall and false-entry constraints.
- **Action-boundary manager** plus chunk-conditioned RL specialists: ownership change clears the queued suffix and re-queries; specialists sample chunks conditioned on latent, proprioception, and a ==reference-dropout== VLA proposal.

## Results

- On LIBERO Object, RouteRLT reaches **92.22%** full-task success versus **85.00%** for the base policy and **91.11%** for privileged-boundary routing, with **14.3%** better successes per 1,000 steps.
- Real-robot insertion rises from **6.7%** to **35.0%**, matching the **33.3%** scripted-height oracle; the oracle reaches the fixture more often (**80.0%** vs **65.0%**) yet converts fewer arrivals (**41.7%** vs **53.8%**).
- Ablations: dropping language from the latent costs full-task success (**90.56%**), and a single full-trajectory specialist trained under matched budgets reaches only **88.89%**; isolated specialists score **83.3%** pickup, **62.1%** insertion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Failures concentrate in a small fraction of the horizon, so localizing RL capacity to those phases beats spreading one specialist and sparse reward across the whole trajectory.
> - A frozen policy's internal representation already encodes enough phase information to replace privileged or operator handoff triggers, provided language tokens are included for object identity.
> - Handoff state distribution matters more than upstream arrival rate: a semantically aligned entry converts better than a geometrically triggered one from a better starting position.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A brief contact-rich stage, such as grasp closure, alignment, or insertion, can determine whether an otherwise competent long-horizon rollout succeeds. In the paper's wire-insertion example, approach and transport can work but grasp closure or final insertion can fail. The authors' response is to specialize *where* extra learning matters, rather than refining the whole trajectory. A broad, pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) policy keeps the parts of a task it already handles. A reinforcement-learning (RL) specialist trained for the precision-critical phase takes over there, and control then returns to the generalist. RouteRLT learns when to make that handoff. On LIBERO Object pick-and-place tasks, this learned routing improves full-task success over the base policy and approaches the privileged-boundary reference.

Table II: method rows compared on full-task success (%), pickup success (%), and successes per 1k steps. SmolVLA@10 reports 85.00 ± 0.00, 85.00 ± 0.00, and 4.97 ± 0.03. Oracle-routed RLT reports 91.11 ± 0.96, 92.22 ± 0.96, and 5.56 ± 0.09. RouteRLT (image + language) reports 92.22 ± 2.55, 92.78 ± 1.92, and 5.68 ± 0.24. These are simulation statistics, not hardware results.

### 2. How the handoff works

The frozen VLA acts as the generalist. An RL specialist is a lightweight RL policy trained for a single precision-critical phase. The closest predecessor, [[2604.23073|RL Token (RLT)]], introduced a compact readout of a frozen VLA representation for a lightweight RL actor-critic. RLT targets a predefined critical phase, with its scope fixed by a signal supplied during data collection. RouteRLT instead learns *which controller owns the current phase* from the VLA's compact internal representation, without any privileged signal at deployment.

The mechanism has three linked parts:

1. A learned token compresses the frozen VLA's internal representation into a compact state, $z_{t}$.
1. A phase selector reads that state over a short causal window and predicts which controller should act. A stabilizer uses separate entry and exit thresholds, plus dwell requirements, to suppress transient switching.
1. When ownership changes, an action-boundary manager discards the unexecuted suffix of the old action chunk and obtains a fresh chunk from the incoming controller. The accepted handoff therefore takes effect at the next action rather than waiting for the old chunk to finish.

Privileged information is still used during training. The specialists are trained using privileged phase boundaries, and routing labels are collected under privileged routing. RouteRLT avoids privileged phase and task state at *deployment*, not throughout training.

### 3. Two decisive comparisons

The evaluation uses three metrics:

- **Full-task success** is the share of evaluation rollouts completing the prescribed pick-and-place task.
- **Pickup success** measures the critical pickup stage.
- **Successes per 1k steps** means completed tasks per 1,000 environment steps. It is an interaction-efficiency measure, not elapsed time, fewer generated tokens, or fewer attempts.

All methods share 60 held-out episodes, task order, and a 300-step horizon at 20 Hz. Values are means ± sample standard deviations over three independently trained seeds.

**Learned versus privileged routing.** The key comparison for a skeptic is routing with privileged boundaries and the same specialist. The direct comparison shares the VLA, encoder, specialist checkpoint, horizons, and initializations. Only the source of controller transitions differs: the oracle uses privileged boundaries, while RouteRLT uses predictions from $z_{t}$. RouteRLT's 92.22% full-task success is close to oracle-routed RLT's 91.11%. This supports the claim that the learned router can recover the benefit of specialist control without deployment access to the privileged boundaries. The reported values and standard deviations do not establish statistical equivalence.

**Handoffs versus one specialist for the whole trajectory.** A single full-trajectory RL specialist under the same interaction and update budgets improves over SmolVLA. It still remains below RouteRLT on full-task success, pickup success, and interaction efficiency (88.89% ± 1.92 full-task success and 5.37 ± 0.13 successes per 1k steps). The authors interpret this as evidence that concentrating RL control on pickup while retaining the VLA for other phases matters, rather than merely adding an RL policy.

### 4. What the result establishes

In simulation, learned routing retains the localized specialist gain near the privileged-boundary result. The hardware test shows automatic selection of pickup and insertion specialists. However, insertion execution begins after operator alignment, so it is not evidence of fully autonomous multi-specialist composition. The hardware results also come from one robot, connector, and port geometry, with grasp pose an uncontrolled source of variation.
