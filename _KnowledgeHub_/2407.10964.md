# No Train, all Gain: Self-Supervised Gradients Improve Deep Frozen Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Amsterdam and valeo.ai introduced FUNGI (Features from UNsupervised GradIents), a method that extracts and combines self-supervised gradients from frozen pretrained models with their original embeddings to create enhanced feature representations. This approach consistently improved kNN classification, retrieval, and dense prediction across various modalities and backbones by up to +17.2% mIoU in semantic segmentation without any additional training of the backbone model.

## Problem

- Enhancing features for non-parametric tasks (e.g., kNN classification, retrieval) often requires extensive feature engineering, fine-tuning, or specialized model adaptations.
- Pretrained deep neural network embeddings may not fully capture all task-relevant information, especially in low-data regimes, without further training.
- Adapting powerful pretrained models to new tasks typically involves expensive fine-tuning or complex architectural modifications, limiting their plug-and-play utility.

## Method

- **FUNGI** extracts gradients from self-supervised loss functions (e.g., ==DINO==, ==SimCLR==, ==KL Divergence==) applied to a specific hidden layer of a *frozen* pretrained backbone.
- The extracted gradients are vectorized, reduced in dimensionality using ==binary random projections==, and ==L2-normalized==.
- These processed gradients are then concatenated with the L2-normalized original embeddings, followed by a final dimensionality reduction via ==PCA== to maintain the original feature size.

## Results

- FUNGI consistently improved kNN classification accuracy across **11** image datasets and **20** Vision Transformer backbones, with notable gains in few-shot settings.
- Achieved substantial gains in retrieval-based semantic segmentation, boosting mIoU by up to **+17.2%** for DINO ViT-B/16 on Pascal VOC 2012, becoming competitive with specialized state-of-the-art models.
- Demonstrated effectiveness across other modalities, improving kNN text classification on **five** datasets using BERT/T5 and enhancing audio classification accuracy on ESC-50 and SpeechCommands V2.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gradients derived from self-supervised objectives, even from frozen pretrained models, contain valuable predictive signals that are complementary to the model's standard embeddings.
> - Combining multiple types of self-supervised gradients often yields more robust and informative representations, as different objectives capture diverse and complementary information.
> - This gradient-based feature enhancement provides a "no train, all gain" method, improving performance across various pretrained models and modalities without updating any model parameters.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This work operates within several established areas of machine learning and computer vision, specifically addressing the enhancement of feature representations for non-parametric tasks.

The **k-nearest neighbor (kNN) algorithm** serves as a foundational component, recognized for its versatility and scalability, with applications extending from traditional image classification to recent advancements in text retrieval and visual in-context learning. Historically, devising effective features for these tasks involved extensive **feature engineering**, ranging from simple local descriptors (e.g., SIFT, HOG) to various mid-level and global pooling techniques. More recently, researchers have coupled off-the-shelf pretrained deep neural network backbones with such pooling strategies to extract strong embeddings. However, these methods often necessitate task-specific expertise and tuning.

The paper distinguishes itself from **fast adaptation** or **meta-learning** approaches, which aim to learn model initializations or generate classifier weights that facilitate quick fine-tuning with limited data. Unlike these methods, the proposed technique, FUNGI, does not require specialized training or updating model parameters. It also differs from **test-time training**, where model parameters are updated during inference using self-supervised objectives to mitigate distribution shifts. FUNGI, in contrast, operates without parameter updates and is applicable to a broader range of retrieval-based tasks.

A significant area of recent progress is **self-supervised learning (SSL)**, which learns robust representations from unlabeled data using various pretext tasks (e.g., contrastive learning, clustering, self-distillation, reconstruction). While traditional SSL focuses on pretraining an encoder, this work utilizes the *gradients* derived from SSL objectives applied to a *frozen* pretrained model. This shifts the focus from learning a new encoder to extracting novel information from an existing one, without requiring human annotations.

The concept of using **gradient-based features** is not entirely new. Previous work has explored kernel regression using empirical neural tangent kernels (eNTK), which involve the model Jacobian, and extracting features from the Jacobian of top convolutional layers for linear classifiers. FUNGI differentiates itself by being annotation-agnostic, computationally more efficient by extracting gradients from a single layer rather than a full Jacobian, and specifically designed for non-parametric retrieval-based tasks rather than primarily predictive models.

In essence, this research introduces a novel form of feature engineering by systematically leveraging self-supervised gradients to enrich existing frozen deep representations, aiming to achieve strong performance without further training or task-specific manual tuning, thereby offering a "plug-and-play" enhancement to a wide range of pretrained models.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce FUNGI (Features from UNsupervised GradIents), a method designed to enhance the feature representations generated by frozen transformer encoders. The central hypothesis is that gradients, which encode information about how a neural network's weights should adjust to solve a given task, contain valuable signals that are complementary to the network's learned embeddings.

The key motivations for developing FUNGI are:
- **Exploiting inherent information in gradients:** Gradients reflect the local curvature of the loss landscape, providing insights into the relationship between input data and the current state of the network. The authors hypothesize that these first-order learning signals, when derived from self-supervised objectives, can adapt to the characteristics of a downstream dataset even without updating the model's parameters.
- **Leveraging self-supervised objectives without annotation:** By using self-supervised losses, FUNGI bypasses the need for human annotations for the gradient extraction process. This allows the method to be applied broadly across diverse datasets and domains where labeled data might be scarce or expensive.
- **Integrating complementary information:** Different self-supervised learning objectives (e.g., contrastive learning, distillation, entropy maximization) are hypothesized to induce gradients that capture distinct types of information. The motivation is that combining these diverse gradient signals, which may mitigate weaknesses of individual objectives, can lead to more robust and informative representations than any single source alone, including the original embeddings. Empirical evidence in the paper, such as CKA similarity metrics and per-class accuracy deltas, supports the notion that different gradients encode varied and complementary information.
- **Achieving strong representations without training or extensive feature engineering:** A core motivation is to simplify the process of obtaining high-quality features. FUNGI aims to improve performance in non-parametric tasks (like kNN classification, clustering, and retrieval) by enhancing existing embeddings in a "plug-and-play" manner, without requiring additional training of the backbone model or specialized, task-dependent feature engineering.
- **Broad applicability and generality:** The method is motivated by the desire for a general solution that can benefit various pretrained backbones across different modalities (vision, text, audio) and diverse pretraining strategies, making it a versatile enhancement tool.

In summary, the authors sought to unlock predictive information latent in self-supervised gradients from frozen models, combine it effectively with existing embeddings, and thereby create stronger, task-agnostic features for retrieval-based applications without the overhead of additional model training.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The FUNGI method enhances feature representations by combining standard neural network embeddings with gradients extracted from self-supervised loss functions applied to a frozen pretrained model. This process involves several key steps: gradient extraction, dimensionality reduction, and concatenation with the original embeddings, followed by a final dimensionality reduction.

#### 3.1. Gradient Extraction

1. **Frozen Backbone and Projection Head:** The process begins with an arbitrary pretrained neural network backbone, `f`, typically a Vision Transformer (ViT) in vision tasks. A randomly initialized linear projection head, `h`, is attached to the backbone.
2. **Latent Representation:** For an input `x`, the latent representation `z = h(f'(x))` is obtained. `f'` denotes the frozen backbone's output.
3. **Self-Supervised Loss Computation:** One or more self-supervised objectives are computed using `z`. The paper primarily focuses on three families of losses:
 - **DINO (Distillation with no labels):** The standard DINO loss is used, which enforces correspondence between global and local crops of an image. In FUNGI's application, both student and teacher models share parameters but have independent projection heads (`h_s` and `h_t`). The objective is `L_DINO = Cross-Entropy(z_s, z_t)`.
 - **SimCLR (A Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning of Visual Representations):** A modified version is employed. Instead of contrasting different augmented views of an image within a batch, 49 overlapping patches are generated for each input image, forming a "positive set." This set is then contrasted against a fixed batch of 49 x 256 negative examples using the InfoNCE loss.
 - **KL Divergence:** This objective calculates the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the softmaxed logits of the latent representation `z` and a uniform distribution `U`. `L_KL = KL(softmax(z) || U)`.
4. **Backpropagation and Gradient Extraction:** After computing the chosen self-supervised loss, backpropagation is performed. The gradients are extracted with respect to the weights and biases of a specific hidden linear layer within the *frozen* backbone `f`. By default, the attention output projection of the last transformer block is selected as the gradient source.

#### 3.2. From Gradients to Retrieval-Friendly Features

1. **Vectorization:** The extracted gradients, typically high-dimensional, are first vectorized by flattening them into a single `m`-dimensional vector.
2. **Binary Random Projections:** To address high dimensionality, computational cost, and the curse of dimensionality for nearest-neighbor retrieval, the vectorized gradients are projected to a lower dimension, `d` (matching the original embedding dimensionality). This is achieved using binary random projections, where the gradient vector is multiplied by a matrix `R ∈ {-1, 1}^(d,m)` whose entries are randomly sampled Bernoulli variables with `p=0.5`. The projected gradient `g_beta(x)` is defined as `R vec(∇L_beta(x))`.

#### 3.3. Combining with Embeddings

1. **L2-Normalization:** Each projected gradient and the original model embedding are individually L2-normalized. This step ensures that each component contributes equally to the final feature representation, as gradient magnitudes can vary significantly across different losses.
2. **Concatenation:** The normalized projected gradients (one or more) are concatenated with the normalized original model embeddings: `ϕ(x) = cat(g'_beta1(x), g'_beta2(x), ..., f'(x))`.
3. **Principal Component Analysis (PCA):** A final dimensionality reduction step is applied using PCA to reduce the concatenated feature vector back to the original embedding dimensionality `d`. This allows the combination of multiple gradient features without increasing storage or retrieval costs. The final FUNGI feature is `ϕ_PCA(x) = PCA_d(ϕ(x))`.

#### 3.4. Application to In-Context Scene Understanding

For retrieval-based in-context scene understanding, FUNGI enhances patch features. A SimCLR loss is specifically implemented for this task, contrasting patch tokens from an input image against their nearest neighbors retrieved from a pre-built memory bank of image patches. This generates per-patch gradients, which are then combined with the original patch embeddings.

This structured approach enables FUNGI to extract task-relevant signals from frozen models via self-supervised gradients and integrate them into a compact, enhanced feature representation suitable for various non-parametric downstream tasks.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents comprehensive empirical evaluations demonstrating the efficacy of FUNGI across diverse tasks, modalities, and backbone architectures.

#### 4.1. Image Classification (kNN)

- **Consistent Improvements:** FUNGI consistently improved kNN classification accuracy across 11 image datasets and 20 Vision Transformer (ViT) backbones, spanning various model sizes (e.g., ViT-S, ViT-B, ViT-L) and pretraining strategies (supervised, DINO, MoCov3, CLIP, EVA-CLIP, MAE, AugReg). Average improvements were observed in both full-dataset and few-shot (e.g., 5-shot) scenarios.
- **Data Efficiency:** FUNGI demonstrated significant performance gains in low-data regimes, particularly when 3 to 6 shots per class were used, highlighting its utility in scenarios with limited labeled data.
- **Complementary Information:** The study confirmed that gradients from different self-supervised objectives possess predictive abilities and encode complementary information to model embeddings. Combining multiple gradients (e.g., KL + DINO + SimCLR) generally yielded greater performance improvements compared to using a single gradient type or embeddings alone.
- **Impact of Pretraining:** The benefits of FUNGI tended to diminish as the size of the pretraining dataset for the backbone increased. For instance, improvements were smaller for backbones pretrained on IN21K compared to IN1K, and for EVA-CLIP (2B text-image pairs) compared to CLIP (400M pairs).

#### 4.2. In-Context Scene Understanding (Semantic Segmentation)

- **Significant Enhancement:** FUNGI substantially boosted the performance of DINO features for retrieval-based semantic segmentation on Pascal VOC 2012 and ADE20K. Improvements in mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) were notable, reaching up to +17.2% for DINO ViT-B/16 on Pascal VOC.
- **Competitive Performance:** A DINO ViT-B/16 model enhanced with FUNGI achieved mIoU scores competitive with the specialized state-of-the-art HummingBird model (within 3.1-3.5%), despite HummingBird's pretraining being tailored for dense prediction tasks. This was achieved without any specific training for FUNGI.
- **Qualitative Improvements:** FUNGI produced segmentation masks that were qualitatively sharper and more complete compared to those generated using raw DINO features.
- **Data-Efficient Segmentation:** In data-efficient settings, FUNGI-enhanced DINO outperformed even end-to-end fine-tuning of DINO on Pascal VOC, demonstrating its effectiveness in low-data dense prediction tasks.

#### 4.3. Other Modalities

- **Natural Language Processing:** FUNGI yielded improvements in kNN text classification across five datasets (TREC, Banking-77, SST, AG News, Tweet-Eval) using BERT and T5 language models. KL gradients generally performed well, while SimCLR gradients occasionally struggled, suggesting the need for modality-specific augmentation strategies.
- **Audio Classification:** For the audio modality, FUNGI improved kNN classification accuracy on ESC-50 (from 42.8% to 47.0%) and SpeechCommands V2 (from 27.4% to 29.9%) using an SSAST backbone, primarily driven by KL gradients.

#### 4.4. Additional Evaluations

- **Image Retrieval:** FUNGI features improved mean average precision (mAP) on the revisited Oxford and Paris landmarks datasets, particularly for CLIP backbones.
- **Linear Classification:** In logistic regression, FUNGI features were most effective for backbones pretrained with supervised objectives, showing less consistent benefits for self-supervised backbones, and in some cases, slight degradations. The optimal feature combination was often backbone-specific.
- **Clustering:** FUNGI features, particularly with KL and DINO gradients, improved the overlap between clusters and classes in k-means clustering, with notable gains on datasets like Oxford-IIT Pets.
- **Language In-Context Learning:** Using FUNGI features to retrieve in-context examples for GPT 4o mini improved classification accuracy by up to 2.5% on Banking-77.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

- **Gradients Source Layer:** Deeper layers within the transformer consistently produced more predictive gradients, with the attention output projection of the last block being a robust and memory-efficient choice.
- **Projection Head:** A randomly initialized linear head combined with L2-normalization of embeddings yielded the most predictive gradients.
- **Dimensionality Reduction:** While random projections had little impact on gradient performance, PCA proved to be the most effective method for the final combined feature vector, often providing minor performance improvements and consistently outperforming random projections for the concatenated feature space.
- **Self-Supervised Objectives for Gradients:** Not all SSL objectives produced equally effective gradients; DINO and KL gradients were generally predictive, whereas gradients from DeepCluster and iBOT (masked image modeling) showed poorer performance.

Overall, the findings indicate that FUNGI is a versatile, no-training method capable of consistently enhancing representations from diverse pretrained models for a range of non-parametric tasks, with particularly strong performance in low-data and retrieval-based scenarios.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The introduction of FUNGI represents a notable contribution to the field of deep learning, particularly in the domain of feature representation and transfer learning.

#### 5.1. Core Significance

- **Novel Feature Enhancement Paradigm:** The work fundamentally demonstrates that gradients derived from self-supervised objectives, applied to frozen pretrained models, possess predictive capabilities and encode information complementary to the models' learned embeddings. This provides a new avenue for extracting valuable signals from existing deep networks without modifying their parameters.
- **"No Train, All Gain" Approach:** FUNGI offers a simple, plug-and-play method to improve feature quality without requiring any additional training of the backbone model or task-specific fine-tuning. This contrasts with many adaptation strategies that necessitate gradient updates or specialized architectural modifications.
- **Generality and Robustness:** The method's effectiveness across a wide array of backbones (diverse sizes, pretraining strategies), modalities (vision, text, audio), and downstream tasks (kNN classification, image retrieval, clustering, in-context learning) underscores its broad applicability and robustness.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Enhanced Non-Parametric Methods:** By consistently boosting the performance of kNN-based approaches, FUNGI can improve the reliability and accuracy of many retrieval-focused applications where non-parametric methods are prevalent due to their simplicity, interpretability, and ability to handle evolving data.
- **Improved In-Context Learning:** The significant gains observed in retrieval-based in-context scene understanding, particularly for semantic segmentation, suggest FUNGI could enhance visual in-context learning systems. By providing richer patch features for retrieval, it enables more accurate dense predictions from pretrained models without task-specific training. This is particularly relevant for adapting models to new tasks with limited annotated examples.
- **Benefits for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG):** The authors explicitly identify a potential positive impact on RAG systems in Natural Language Processing. By improving the quality of retrieved text chunks through enhanced text features, FUNGI could contribute to more reliable and factual language model generations, addressing current challenges in grounding and hallucination.
- **Efficiency in Low-Data Regimes:** FUNGI's strong performance in few-shot scenarios across image, text, and dense prediction tasks highlights its utility in domains where labeled data is scarce or expensive, offering a resource-efficient way to adapt powerful pretrained models.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The findings may stimulate further investigation into the information content of gradients, leading to new methods for feature engineering, model introspection, and adaptation without explicit training. This could involve exploring other types of gradients, different self-supervised objectives, or more sophisticated ways of combining gradient information.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

While broadly effective, the paper notes that FUNGI introduces a computational overhead during feature extraction, particularly for SimCLR and DINO losses, although retrieval speed remains unaffected after dimensionality reduction. Additionally, the benefits of FUNGI tend to diminish for backbones pretrained on extremely large datasets. Future work is suggested to explore optimal self-supervised objectives and data augmentation strategies tailored for text and audio modalities, where current SimCLR gradients sometimes perform inconsistently.

In conclusion, FUNGI offers a practical and broadly applicable approach to leverage intrinsic signals within frozen deep models, providing consistent performance enhancements across diverse applications and modalities. Its simplicity and effectiveness without additional training make it a valuable tool for extracting more informative features from existing pretrained representations.
