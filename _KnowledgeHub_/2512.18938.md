# A Framework for Deploying Learning-based Quadruped Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Open reproducible pipeline for training, benchmarking, and deploying RL whole-body controllers on Unitree B1+Z1 quadruped-manipulator, unifying sim-to-sim (Isaac Gym to MuJoCo via ROS hardware abstraction) and sim-to-real transfer; whole-body control extends reach and improves teleoperated object-picking success over floating-base baselines.

## Problem

- Most RL whole-body control frameworks for loco-manipulation remain proprietary and hardware-specific, limiting reproducibility and systematic evaluation across research groups.
- No open end-to-end RL pipeline supports whole-body control or sim-to-real deployment on the widely used Unitree B1+Z1 quadruped-manipulator platform.
- Existing partially open-sourced codebases mostly omit real-hardware deployment stacks, hindering rigorous replication and benchmarking of loco-manipulation controllers.

## Method

- **MujocoHW** interface emulates the ==hardware_interface::RobotHW== abstraction so the same ==ROS== policy and controller stack runs unmodified across Isaac Gym training, MuJoCo simulation, and real hardware.
- Reconstructed Unitree B1+Z1 ==URDF== assets into MuJoCo scenes since no public ==sim-to-sim== pipeline existed, enabling cross-simulator contact-model comparison.
- ==Foot contact estimation== via ==Jacobian-transpose pseudo-inverse== of joint torques with force clamping and thresholding, since B1 lacks foot sensors, running at 500 Hz for stance detection.

## Results

- Isaac Gym showed elevated yaw RMSE (**0.231** rad/s low-pose) from rigid contact oscillations, while MuJoCo default friction gave smoother but drifting tracking (**0.113-0.123** m/s).
- Teleoperated picking: WBC achieved near-perfect success across object heights (e.g. **3/3** at 0.18m) versus floating-base baseline failing entirely at low heights (**0/3** at 0.18m).
- Mid end-effector pose gave most stable real-robot locomotion at max teleoperation speed (**0.4 m/s**, **0.8 rad/s**), while high pose induced base shaking matching Isaac Gym oscillation trends.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sim-to-sim transfer exposes simulator-specific contact-model artifacts (Isaac Gym oscillation vs MuJoCo drift) that a single simulator's benchmark would hide entirely.
> - Tuned-friction MuJoCo results can look artificially robust; apparent smoothness may reflect unrealistic traction tuning rather than true policy generalization.
> - Whole-body coordination meaningfully extends manipulator reachability over floating-base control, but grasp success still bottlenecks on object geometry and gripper hardware limits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards more agile and versatile mobile manipulators, particularly quadruped robots equipped with robotic arms. These platforms combine the terrain adaptability of legged robots with the dexterity of manipulators, holding potential for applications in complex environments such as disaster response, rough terrain traversal, and stair climbing. However, controlling these high-dimensional systems, such as the Unitree B1+Z1 with 19 degrees of freedom (DoF), presents a significant challenge.

Historically, control strategies for legged mobile manipulators have often decoupled locomotion and manipulation. While simplifying control, this approach limits the coordination between the base and the arm, potentially restricting the system's overall capabilities and generalization. Whole-body control (WBC) is an alternative paradigm that seeks to unify all DoF, allowing the legs to actively extend the arm's workspace and the arm to contribute to the robot's balance.

Within low-level WBC, several categories of approaches exist. Optimization-based methods, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC), can generate multi-contact behaviors but typically require accurate models and are computationally intensive. Sampling-based methods, including Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) and contact planning, address hybrid contacts but often remain task-specific and computationally demanding.

More recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising avenue for WBC. RL-based approaches have demonstrated the capacity to generate unified policies that achieve smoother coordination, extended reach, and improved robustness against disturbances. However, a significant limitation within the current landscape of RL-based loco-manipulation frameworks is their proprietary nature and hardware specificity. Many existing pipelines are not openly accessible, hindering reproducibility, systematic evaluation, and broader community engagement. Specifically, there has been no open end-to-end RL pipeline supporting whole-body control and sim-to-real deployment on the Unitree B1+Z1, a platform that is gaining traction in research. This work addresses this gap by presenting an open and transparent framework for training, benchmarking, and deploying RL-based controllers on this particular quadruped-manipulator system.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and present an open, transparent, and extensible pipeline for research in whole-body reinforcement learning for legged mobile manipulators. This pipeline is designed to facilitate reproducible training, sim-to-sim benchmarking, and deployment on real hardware, specifically targeting the Unitree B1 quadruped equipped with a Z1 arm.

The motivation behind this work stems from several limitations observed in the current state of RL-based loco-manipulation research:

1. **Lack of Open and Reproducible Frameworks:** A significant portion of existing RL-based loco-manipulation frameworks are proprietary. This lack of transparency impedes external researchers from replicating results, comparing methodologies, and systematically evaluating proposed solutions. The absence of open-source tools restricts the pace of innovation and collaboration within the field.
2. **Hardware-Specific Implementations:** Many RL control frameworks are tailored to specific robotic platforms, making their transferability and generalization to different hardware challenging. This necessitates significant re-engineering efforts for each new robot system, slowing down research progress.
3. **Absence of a Unified Sim-to-Real Pipeline for Unitree B1+Z1:** Despite the increasing popularity of the Unitree B1+Z1 as a research platform, an open end-to-end RL pipeline that supports whole-body control and robust sim-to-real deployment on this specific robot has been unavailable. This gap limits the ability of researchers to leverage the platform's capabilities with advanced RL control strategies.
4. **Challenges in Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Successfully transferring policies learned in simulation to physical robots remains a complex problem. Discrepancies between simulation environments (e.g., contact models) and reality can lead to performance degradation or failure on real hardware. A robust deployment framework is needed to systematically analyze and mitigate these transfer challenges.
5. **Need for Benchmarking and Systematic Evaluation:** The proprietary nature of many systems also hinders standardized benchmarking. An open pipeline can serve as a common foundation for evaluating and comparing different RL algorithms and control strategies under consistent conditions.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to provide a foundational toolset that can accelerate the development and application of robust, real-world RL-based loco-manipulation capabilities. The planned open-source release of this pipeline is intended to foster community engagement and support future advancements in the field.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research focuses on developing a unified framework for training, benchmarking, and deploying reinforcement learning (RL) based whole-body controllers on a quadruped mobile manipulator.

**Hardware Platform:**
The framework is built around the Unitree B1 quadruped robot, which is equipped with a Unitree Z1 arm mounted on its torso. This configuration provides 19 degrees of freedom (DoFs): 12 DoFs for the B1's legs (hip, thigh, knee for each of four legs) and 6 DoFs for the Z1 arm, plus a 1-DoF gripper.

**Low-Level Policy Architecture and Training:**
The authors adopt and extend a partially open-source framework from Liu et al. as their baseline for the low-level policy.

- **RL Formulation:** The controller is formulated as an RL policy within a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The state space (S) includes proprioception, joint states, and contact information. Actions (A) are defined as joint targets. The policy aims to maximize a discounted return through a reward function (r). Partial observability is addressed by incorporating observation histories, recurrent encoders, and domain randomization during training.
- **Policy Architecture:** The core low-level policy ($\pi_\theta$) is a three-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), each layer consisting of 128 units with ELU activation. The output head uses a tanh activation to produce 12 leg joint targets. Inputs to the policy include concatenated proprioceptive information, the previous action, gait phase cues, a high-level command (c_t), and a latent extrinsic vector (z_t). The high-level command specifies base velocities (v_cmd, ω_cmd) and end-effector pose goals (p_ee,cmd, R_ee,cmd). The latent extrinsic vector, which accounts for dynamics variations, is supervised with privileged parameters during training and inferred online via an adaptation module during deployment. Arm control is managed analytically using damped least-squares Inverse Kinematics (IK), allowing the RL policy to focus on coordinated body behaviors. Policy outputs are normalized leg joint positions, which are then mapped to admissible ranges and tracked by onboard PD controllers.
- **Optimization:** The policies are trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an on-policy RL algorithm. The PPO objective combines a clipped surrogate objective with a value loss and entropy regularization to encourage exploration. Advantages are estimated using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). Training is performed in Isaac Gym, a high-performance GPU-based physics simulator, which enables large-batch on-policy updates across thousands of parallel environments.
- **Training Setup:** The reward function integrates multiple objectives, including base-velocity tracking, energy efficiency, stability (alive bonus), and gait regularization, using exponential kernels for velocity tracking stabilization. Commands for base velocities and end-effector poses are sampled from wide ranges. To facilitate sim-to-real transfer, domain randomization is applied across various parameters such as terrain friction and geometry, robot physical parameters (mass, Center of Mass, damping, latency), and sensor noise. A curriculum learning approach gradually increases the difficulty of this randomization. The latent extrinsic vector (z_t) supports online adaptation to unmodeled dynamics.

**Deployment:**
A core contribution of the work is a unified deployment pipeline implemented in the Robot Operating System (ROS) using `ros_control`. This architecture ensures thread safety and real-time low-level control performance.

- **Layered Architecture:** The framework is structured into five layers to ensure consistency between simulation and real-world deployment (Fig. 4).
 1. **Consistency Layer:** Enforces identical nodes, parameters, and variant definitions across simulation and hardware.
 2. **External Interfaces:** Provides teleoperation capabilities (e.g., joystick) and development tools (RViz, Foxglove, RQT Dynamic Reconfigure) for debugging and real-time tuning.
 3. **Core Shared Software Stack:** Contains the policy, a finite state machine, goal management, and unified PD controllers, all of which remain invariant between environments.
 4. **Execution Branching:** This layer diverges for simulation and real hardware.
 - **Simulation Environment:** Integrates MuJoCo physics with a `MujocoHW` emulation layer and data collection utilities.
 - **Real Hardware Environment:** Connects directly via a hardware interface to the B1 and Z1 robot, including safety systems.
- **Sim-to-Sim Verification (`MujocoHW`):** Since no public sim-to-sim pipeline existed for the Unitree B1+Z1, the authors reconstructed the robot's assets from its Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) to generate corresponding MuJoCo scenes (Fig. 5). A `MujocoHW` interface was developed to emulate the `hardware_interface::RobotHW` abstraction, allowing the identical policy and control stack to run in MuJoCo. This interface consists of:
 - A low-level MuJoCo engine simulating rigid-body dynamics and publishing raw sensor streams.
 - A higher-level `MujocoHW` component mapping these streams to standard ROS control interfaces (e.g., `JointStateInterface`, `ImuSensorInterface`, `ContactSensorInterface`).
 - Controller outputs are translated back into MuJoCo commands.
 - A "fake arm" module is available for B1-only configurations, generating synthetic arm states via inverse kinematics to maintain interface consistency.
 - The observation-action contract from Isaac Gym is preserved. Simulation physics runs at 200 Hz, and the ROS control loop at 500 Hz to match real-robot timing.
- **Sim-to-Real Deployment (`QmHW`):** For hardware deployment, the control stack interfaces with the Unitree B1+Z1 via the `QmHW` module, implemented in C++ using `ros_control`. This module bridges ROS controllers with the Unitree SDKs over UDP, exposing standard ROS control abstractions at 500 Hz.
- **Foot Contact Estimation:** The B1 robot lacks direct foot sensors. Contact forces are estimated from joint torques ($\tau$) and robot dynamics using the SVD-based pseudo-inverse of the Jacobian (J) to compute F_contact = (J^T)^-1 τ. A binary contact flag is set when the vertical component of the force (|F_z|) exceeds 80 N, consistent with the B1's load specifications. This estimation runs at 500 Hz for stable stance detection.
- **Teleoperation and Visualization:** A dual-mode joystick control system (Fig. 8) is implemented for teleoperation. In "base mode," joystick inputs map to body velocity commands (v_x, v_y, v_yaw). In "arm mode," inputs incrementally update the end-effector pose in the trunk frame. End-effector goals are smoothly updated and constrained within workspace bounds. The framework integrates with Foxglove Studio for real-time monitoring and visualization of various robot states, including joint states, IMU data, contact events, and end-effector goals (Fig. 9). Adaptive filtering and compression ensure responsiveness without affecting the control loop.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the deployed whole-body policy across three experimental settings: sim-to-sim tracking (Isaac Gym vs. MuJoCo), sim-to-real tracking on the physical Unitree B1+Z1, and real-world teleoperated object picking. The same ROS stack and policy are utilized in all experiments, with only the hardware interface varying.

#### 4.1. Sim-to-sim Tracking

This experiment evaluated the consistency of policy behavior across Isaac Gym and MuJoCo under identical inputs. Three fixed end-effector (EE) poses (low, mid/neutral, high) and a 23-second sequence of locomotion motions (forward, backward, turning, combined) were used. Tracking accuracy was quantified by the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between commanded and measured base velocities (v_x, v_yaw).

- **Isaac Gym:** The policy tracked commanded velocities but exhibited systematic errors. Backward motions generally resulted in the largest translational errors. The low EE pose consistently produced elevated yaw noise, especially during turning and combined motions. Visual analysis of the low configuration revealed step-like artifacts in v_x traces and persistent high-frequency yaw oscillations (Fig. 11). This behavior was attributed to the arm's low position shifting the center of mass and distorting the support geometry, leading to asymmetric loading and unstable ground reactions under PhysX's rigid impulse-based contact model (Fig. 12).
- **MuJoCo (default friction):** In contrast to Isaac Gym, MuJoCo produced smoother dynamics with fewer oscillations and more uniform performance across EE poses. Even in the low configuration, both v_x and yaw trajectories closely followed commands without significant instability. However, visual inspection revealed foot sliding artifacts, which were attributed to MuJoCo's softened constraint solver (Fig. 13).
- **MuJoCo (tuned friction):** To investigate the effect of contact modeling, MuJoCo was evaluated with an artificially enlarged impulse ratio (`impratio=100`). This tuning almost eliminated sliding and resulted in near-zero error during neutral/stop phases (Table 2). The authors concluded that this apparent smoothness was due to unrealistic tangential impedance rather than intrinsic policy robustness.
- **Cross-simulator Comparison (Table 3):** Two consistent findings emerged. First, the low EE pose was the most error-prone configuration, while the high EE pose generally yielded the lowest RMSE across both simulators. Second, absolute error levels were significantly influenced by the contact models of the simulators: Isaac Gym exhibited yaw oscillations, while MuJoCo displayed foot drift. The tuned MuJoCo environment showed artifactually low errors due to unrealistic contact parameters.

#### 4.2. Sim-to-real Tracking

On the physical Unitree B1+Z1 platform, the deployed policy successfully executed locomotion across all tested end-effector (EE) configurations, demonstrating generalization consistent with simulation trends.

- **Mid-pose:** This configuration yielded the most stable behavior, characterized by smooth trajectories and minimal oscillations, even at maximum teleoperation speeds (0.4 m/s, 0.8 rad/s).
- **High-pose:** This setting induced observable base shaking, which the authors suggested might be due to over-compensation for ground contacts, echoing the oscillatory behavior observed in Isaac Gym.
- **Low-pose:** The robot maintained stability but exhibited reduced gait fluidity and a slower forward speed, likely due to the tilted base geometry and reduced ground clearance. Unlike in Isaac Gym, persistent oscillations were not observed in the physical robot for this pose, indicating differences in real-world dynamics compared to the simulation.
- **Reaching Range:** Manual arm adjustments confirmed that the controller adapted the trunk posture to extend the manipulation reach while preserving stability (Fig. 14).

#### 4.3. Real-world Teleoperated Object Picking

Object-picking experiments were conducted using a teleoperated setup with YCB objects (mustard container, potted meat can, plum) placed at various heights (0-0.58 m) and distances (0.5-1.5 m) relative to the robot (Fig. 15). Two teleoperation modes were compared: a "Baseline" using Unitree's default controller with separate arm-base control, and "WBC" (Whole-Body Control) utilizing the unified policy coordinating arm and base movements.

- **Performance Comparison (Table 4):**
 - For high objects (0.58 m), both the baseline and WBC controllers performed similarly.
 - At lower heights (0.18 m and ground level), the baseline controller consistently failed to pick objects due to reach limitations, as its fixed trunk posture restricted the arm's workspace.
 - The WBC policy achieved near-perfect success rates across all tested heights by dynamically adapting the robot's body posture and orientation, effectively extending its reach and improving stability (Fig. 16).
 - Spherical objects, such as the plum, remained challenging for both control modes, particularly at lower heights. This was attributed primarily to the limitations of the gripper and object geometry rather than the control strategy itself, with some instances of slipping observed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a significant contribution to the field of robotic loco-manipulation by addressing a crucial gap in the development and deployment of learning-based controllers. Its primary significance lies in prioritizing reproducibility and transferability over algorithmic novelty, thereby laying a foundational infrastructure for future research.

The potential impact of this research is multi-faceted:

1. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Transparency:** By providing an open and transparent pipeline for training, sim-to-sim benchmarking, and sim-to-real deployment on the Unitree B1+Z1, the work directly counters the prevalent issue of proprietary and hardware-specific RL frameworks. This initiative will enable other researchers to reproduce findings, validate new algorithms, and conduct comparative studies under standardized conditions, fostering a more collaborative and accountable research environment.
2. **Accelerated Research and Development:** The open-source release of this framework will provide a readily available and verified platform for developing and testing novel RL-based loco-manipulation algorithms. This can significantly reduce the overhead associated with setting up complex control systems from scratch, allowing researchers to focus more on algorithmic innovation and exploration of advanced whole-body coordination strategies.
3. **Improved Understanding of Sim-to-Real Challenges:** The detailed cross-simulator studies provide valuable insights into the discrepancies arising from different physics engines' contact models (e.g., Isaac Gym's rigid impulses versus MuJoCo's softened constraints). This analysis highlights contact modeling as a critical bottleneck in achieving faithful sim-to-real transfer and offers guidance for future simulation design and domain randomization strategies. Understanding these differences is crucial for developing more robust and transferable policies.
4. **Demonstrated Capabilities of Whole-Body Control:** The real-world teleoperated object-picking experiments conclusively demonstrate the advantages of coordinated whole-body control. By allowing the robot's base to actively adjust its posture and orientation, the framework shows a significant extension of the arm's reachability and improved stability during manipulation tasks, especially for objects at extreme positions, compared to traditional decoupled or floating-base approaches. This validates the practical benefits of unifying locomotion and manipulation control.
5. **Foundation for Future Advanced Applications:** With a robust and deployable framework in place, future research can build upon this foundation to explore more complex loco-manipulation tasks, such as navigation in highly cluttered environments, dexterous manipulation with dynamic rebalancing, or human-robot collaboration. The identified bottlenecks, particularly in contact modeling and general robustness, also serve as clear directions for future research efforts in RL and robotics.

In summary, this work provides a practical, open-source solution that not only facilitates current research on legged mobile manipulators but also establishes a critical infrastructure for the long-term advancement of learning-based whole-body control, making advanced robotics more accessible and reproducible.
