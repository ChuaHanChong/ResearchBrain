# LiLa-WAM: Lightweight Latent Reasoning World-Action Model for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> LILA-WAM, a lightweight latent-reasoning world-action model for robotic manipulation, enables efficient end-to-end training on a single 24GB GPU. It achieved average success rates of 90.48% on RoboTwin 2.0 and 97.1% on LIBERO, performing comparably to larger state-of-the-art models with significantly fewer parameters.

## Problem

- Existing World-Action Models (WAMs) for robotic manipulation incur high computational and memory costs due to large pre-trained models or multi-stage training pipelines.
- The substantial computational overhead limits accessibility, preventing researchers with modest hardware from effectively training and deploying WAMs.
- Pre-training objectives of general-purpose backbones in WAMs often mismatch the fine-grained spatial and geometric cues critical for precise robotic control tasks.

## Method

- **LILA-WAM** introduces a lightweight, end-to-end trainable WAM that unifies future-state prediction and action generation into a single stream, leveraging a ==frozen DINOv3 visual encoder==.
- The model predicts future states in a ==compact latent representation==, avoiding the computational redundancy of pixel-level synthesis and heavy generative backbones.
- A ==Visual Transition Token (VTT)== is proposed for language-free task specification, encoding tasks as scene transition directions without requiring text or explicit goal images during deployment.

## Results

- LILA-WAM achieved an average success rate of **90.48%** on RoboTwin 2.0 and **97.1%** on LIBERO, matching or exceeding larger models while utilizing **10-16 times** fewer parameters (**0.5B** total).
- Ablation studies demonstrated that removing foresight loss (LffL_{ff}Lff) resulted in a substantial **15.6 percentage point** drop in average success rate (from **70.0%** to **54.4%**) in simulation.
- In real-robot experiments across four manipulation tasks, LILA-WAM achieved an **82.0%** average success rate, showing an **8.0 percentage point** improvement compared to a variant without foresight supervision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit foresight supervision, integrated directly into the action expert, significantly enhances robotic manipulation performance, as evidenced by an 8.0 percentage point improvement in real-robot tasks.
> - A compact latent space, jointly shaped by action generation and future state prediction, can achieve high performance in robotic control with substantially fewer parameters than larger, general-purpose models.
> - Language-free task specification via Visual Transition Tokens (VTTs) provides an effective and efficient alternative to traditional language instructions, focusing attention on task-relevant objects while reducing computational overhead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically focusing on World-Action Models (WAMs). WAMs represent a paradigm for robotic control that extends beyond reactive policies by enabling models to anticipate how a scene will evolve under the robot's actions. This predictive capability is intended to enhance robust control in manipulation tasks.

Existing WAMs broadly fall into two categories: pixel-space methods and latent-space methods. Pixel-space methods generate explicit future observations, such as images or videos. However, these methods often allocate significant computational resources to visual details that may not be directly relevant to the control task, leading to high computational and memory costs during training and inference. Latent-space methods predict future states in a compressed latent representation, avoiding the redundancy of pixel-level synthesis. Despite this, many current latent-space WAMs employ multi-stage training pipelines where key components are trained separately before integration with the policy, thereby increasing the overall training burden and computational requirements.

A common characteristic of many existing WAMs, regardless of their prediction space, is their reliance on large-scale pre-trained models. These can include vision-language models (VLMs) or video-generation backbones. A substantial portion of the parameters in such models may be dedicated to functionalities (e.g., language modeling or general video synthesis) that are not tightly coupled with the fine-grained spatial and geometric cues essential for precise robotic control. This leads to substantial computational overhead and limits accessibility for researchers with modest computational budgets.

LiLa-WAM addresses these limitations by proposing a lightweight, end-to-end trainable WAM designed to operate efficiently under constrained computational resources, such as a single 24GB GPU. It differentiates itself from pixel-space methods by predicting future states in a compact latent space, avoiding the generation of unnecessary visual details. Compared to other latent-space methods, LiLa-WAM integrates future-state prediction and action generation into a single, unified stream, eliminating the complexities and costs associated with multi-stage training pipelines or auxiliary alignment losses. Furthermore, it moves beyond language-based task specification by introducing a language-free visual task representation, offering an alternative that is computationally less demanding and does not require explicit goal images during deployment. This positions LiLa-WAM as a contribution towards more accessible, efficient, and control-relevant robot learning architectures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The development of LiLa-WAM is motivated by several challenges observed in current World-Action Models (WAMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation:

1. **High Computational Overhead of Existing WAMs**: Current WAMs, whether operating in pixel space or latent space, typically incur substantial computational costs. Pixel-space methods expend considerable capacity on generating visual details (e.g., texture, lighting, background) that may not be directly relevant to the control task, leading to expensive training and inference. Latent-space methods, while avoiding pixel synthesis, often rely on multi-stage training pipelines, where different modules are trained independently and then integrated, further increasing the training complexity and resource demands.
2. **Limited Accessibility under Modest Computational Budgets**: The high computational requirements of existing WAMs make them difficult to train and deploy using modest hardware, such as a single GPU. This restricts access for many research groups and practitioners, hindering broader development and experimentation in the field.
3. **Mismatch between Pretraining Objectives and Robotic Control Needs**: Many WAMs are built upon large-scale pre-trained models (e.g., vision-language models, video generation backbones). The pretraining objectives of these backbones (e.g., next-token prediction, image-text contrastive alignment) often emphasize high-level semantics or pixel-level dynamics, which may not align optimally with the fine-grained spatial and geometric cues necessary for precise robotic manipulation. This can lead to a substantial portion of the model's capacity and computation being allocated to information less relevant for control.
4. **Desire for Language-Free Task Specification**: Traditional robotic policies often rely on language instructions for task specification, which introduces text-processing overhead. There is a motivation to explore more lightweight, language-free alternatives that do not require explicit text input or goal images at test time, thereby simplifying deployment and potentially improving efficiency.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the LiLa-WAM research are:

1. **Develop a Lightweight and Computationally Efficient World-Action Model**: To design a WAM that can reason about future states and generate actions with significantly reduced computational overhead, making it trainable end-to-end on a single 24 GB GPU.
2. **Integrate Future-State Prediction and Action Generation**: To unify these two objectives within a single, shared latent space and a single-stream architecture. This is intended to allow the objectives to jointly shape a compact latent space that is both foresight-aware and well-aligned with control requirements, without resorting to multi-stage training or complex auxiliary losses.
3. **Propose a Language-Free Task Representation**: To introduce the Visual Transition Token (VTT), a novel method for task specification that encodes each task as a transition direction in visual feature space. This objective aims to provide a lightweight alternative to language instructions or goal images, focusing on the changes a task brings to the scene.
4. **Demonstrate Competitive Performance**: To empirically evaluate LiLa-WAM across various simulation and real-world manipulation benchmarks, showcasing its effectiveness and competitiveness against larger, more computationally intensive policies despite its lightweight design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LiLa-WAM is designed as a lightweight, latent-reasoning world-action model for robotic manipulation, aiming for end-to-end training on a single 24 GB GPU.

**Problem Formulation:**
The model learns a conditional distribution $p_{\theta}(a_{t:t+H}, \hat{z}_{t+\Delta} | o_t, q_t, c_{\tau})$, where $a_{t:t+H}$ is the predicted action chunk over horizon $H$, $\hat{z}_{t+\Delta}$ is the predicted compact latent representation of the future state after a look-ahead interval $\Delta$, $o_t$ is the current image observation, $q_t$ is the proprioceptive state, and $c_{\tau}$ is a visual task cue. The future state is predicted as a compact latent within the action expert itself, avoiding heavy generative backbones.

**Foresight-Aware Action Expert:**
The core of LiLa-WAM is the Foresight-Aware Action Expert, built upon a frozen DINOv3-ViT-L/16 encoder. DINOv3 is chosen for its dense, self-supervised pretraining that yields patch features rich in fine-grained visual detail, which is considered more relevant for precise manipulation than high-level semantics from general VLMs.

1. **Visual Compression**: The dense patch features from the DINOv3 encoder are compressed into a smaller set of tokens. Specifically, features from the 17th and 21st blocks of DINOv3 are concatenated along the channel dimension and projected to the expert's hidden width $D$ using a linear layer. This multi-level feature fusion aims to capture complementary information at different granularities (low-level spatial detail and high-level semantics). These fused features are then processed by a 64-query, 4-layer adapter (A), which uses learnable queries to cross-attend to the patch features, yielding a compressed visual latent $Z_v \in \mathbb{R}^{N_q \times D}$, where $N_q$ is the number of queries. This fixed-size latent space decouples computational cost from input resolution.
2. **Action Expert Architecture**: The action expert is a 12-layer Diffusion-Transformer (DiT) block. At each flow-matching step, its input is a single token sequence formed by concatenating:
 - Noised action chunk embedding $Z_a \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times D}$.
 - Proprioceptive state embedding $Z_q$.
 - Visual Transition Token (VTT) derived task token $c_{\tau}$.
 - Compressed visual latent $Z_v$.
 The DiT blocks process this sequence using bidirectional self-attention, allowing information exchange between all token groups.
3. **Outputs**: After the final DiT block, a linear head outputs the predicted action velocity $\hat{v}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times d_a}$ (where $d_a$ is the action dimension) from the action token positions. Concurrently, the outputs corresponding to the observation-side tokens form the predicted future latent $\hat{z}_{t+\Delta} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_q \times D}$.
4. **Future-State Prediction Supervision**: During training, $\hat{z}_{t+\Delta}$ is decoded back into the DINOv3 feature space by a lightweight 4-layer Q-Former-style decoder (D). This decoded feature is then supervised against the ground-truth patch features extracted by the frozen DINOv3 encoder from the future observation $o_{t+\Delta}$. This setup allows future prediction to serve as an auxiliary objective to shape the shared latent space without requiring a pixel-level decoder or heavy generative modules. The decoder is discarded during inference, adding no test-time overhead.

**Visual Transition Token (VTT):**
The VTT is a language-free task representation designed to specify tasks without text or goal images at test time. For a given task $\tau$, the VTT $u_{\tau}$ is computed offline as the mean difference between the global image embeddings of the initial and final frames across all demonstration episodes $E_{\tau}$.
$u_{\tau} = \frac{1}{|E_{\tau}|} \sum_{e \in E_{\tau}} (g_e^T - g_e^0)$, where $g_e^0$ and $g_e^T$ are DINOv3 global image embeddings of the first and last frames of episode $e$.
This VTT captures the "transition direction" or what the task changes about the scene. It is then projected to the expert width using a lightweight MLP and injected into the action expert as the task token $c_{\tau}$.

**Training Objective:**
LiLa-WAM is trained end-to-end with a combined loss: $L = L_{fm} + \lambda_{ff} L_{ff}$.

1. **Flow-Matching Loss ($L_{fm}$)**: This loss is used for action-chunk prediction. It involves sampling a noise $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and a flow time $s \in$. A linear interpolation $x_s = (1-s)x_0 + sx_1$ is formed, where $x_1$ is the ground-truth action chunk. The expert $v_{\theta}$ is trained to predict the constant target velocity $v^* = x_1 - x_0$.
 $L_{fm} = E_{x_0, s} || \hat{v}_s - v^* ||_2^2$.
2. **Foresight Loss ($L_{ff}$)**: This loss supervises future-state prediction. It is a per-token cosine loss between the features decoded from the predicted future latent $\hat{z}_{t+\Delta}$ and the patch features extracted by DINOv3 from the ground-truth future observation $o_{t+\Delta}$.
$\lambda_{ff}$ is a scalar coefficient (set to 0.5) balancing the two loss terms.

**Implementation Details:**
The policy takes a 16-dimensional proprioceptive state and predicts a 14-dimensional action sequence with a chunk length of 32, executing the first 16 steps. The camera resolution is 320x240. The future horizon $\Delta$ is set equal to the action chunk length (32). The model has 0.5B parameters (0.2B trainable, 0.3B frozen). Training uses AdamW optimizer, a batch size of 128, and a stage-wise cosine learning-rate schedule. Joint training over 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks requires approximately 110 GPU hours on an NVIDIA RTX 5090. Inference takes 85 ms on an NVIDIA RTX 4090.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

LiLa-WAM was evaluated across various simulation benchmarks (RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO) and real-robot tasks, demonstrating its effectiveness and efficiency.

**Overall Performance in Simulation:**

- **RoboTwin 2.0 (50 tasks)**: LiLa-WAM achieved an average success rate of 90.48% in the clean setting and 89.04% in the randomized setting. This performance is competitive with, and in some cases surpasses, larger state-of-the-art models. For instance, it outperformed the 8B Motus (88.66% clean) and 5B GigaWorld-Policy (86.36% clean) by 1.8 and 4.1 percentage points respectively, while utilizing 16x and 10x fewer parameters (0.5B total). It also slightly exceeded Being-H0.7 (3B) by 0.3 points in the clean setting.
- **LIBERO (4 suites, 40 tasks)**: LiLa-WAM achieved a 97.1% average success rate across the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites. This matched the performance of OpenVLA-OFT, which uses approximately 14 times more parameters (7B). Among lightweight models, LiLa-WAM surpassed EVO-1 (0.8B) by 2.3 points and SmolVLA (2B) by 8.3 points. These results indicate that LiLa-WAM can achieve high performance with a significantly smaller model footprint and single-GPU training budget.

**Ablation Studies (on 10 representative RoboTwin 2.0 tasks):**

- **Foresight Supervision (L_ff)**: Removing the foresight loss ($L_{ff}$) from the training objective caused a substantial drop in average success rate from 70.0% to 54.4%. This finding highlights the critical contribution of explicit future-state prediction to the model's performance.
- **Task Conditioning (VTT vs. CLIP-language)**: Replacing the Visual Transition Token (VTT) with CLIP-encoded language instructions resulted in an 8.6 percentage point decrease in average success rate (from 70.0% to 61.4%). This suggests that the VTT provides a viable and effective lightweight form of task specification.
- **Visual Backbone (DINOv3 vs. Qwen3VL)**: Using a larger Qwen3VL-2B backbone with language conditioning achieved 61.0% success, which is lower than LiLa-WAM's DINOv3 backbone with VTT (70.0%), despite Qwen3VL being over 4 times larger. This indicates that a larger pre-trained backbone does not automatically guarantee better control performance, and DINOv3's fine-grained features are well-suited for this control task.
- **Number of Adapter Queries ($N_q$)**: An optimal number of 64 adapter queries yielded the best performance (70.0%), with smaller counts (8 or 32) leading to performance degradation, likely due to a loss of spatial detail. Larger counts (96) also showed a slight performance drop with increased memory usage, suggesting diminishing returns.
- **Choice of DINOv3 Feature Layers**: Combining features from the 17th and 21st DINOv3 blocks resulted in the highest success rate (70.0%). Using only later blocks (e.g., 24th or 23rd) or only the 21st block led to lower performance, suggesting that mid-level layers provide complementary information essential for fine-grained manipulation by preserving object locations and local geometry.

**Attention Analysis:**

- **VTT vs. Action Tokens Attention**: Visualizations of attention maps revealed a division of labor. The VTT primarily focused on the objects acted upon and the target areas of the task (e.g., fan and its pad), providing task-level context. In contrast, action tokens concentrated attention on the robot's arm and gripper, and the immediate manipulation region, indicating a focus on how the manipulation is executed.
- **Per-layer Attention Dynamics**: Across different DiT blocks, the VTT consistently activated on both the object to be grasped and its destination, while action tokens more narrowly focused on the currently manipulated object.
- **Action Conditioning Probe**: Perturbing action tokens by replacing the action chunk at later denoising stages (after s ≈ 0.5) caused a notable drop in the cosine similarity of predicted future features, confirming that the future-state tokens encode action-conditioned dynamics and are sensitive to action choices. Masking visual-to-action attention pathways also led to a performance decrease (from 70.0% to 64.8%), further supporting the importance of action information flow for dynamics-aware prediction.

**Real-Robot Experiments:**

- On an Agilex Piper 6-DoF robotic arm across four manipulation tasks, LiLa-WAM achieved an average success rate of 82.0%. A variant of LiLa-WAM without the foresight loss ($L_{ff}$) achieved 74.0%. This 8.0 percentage point improvement (ranging from 4 to 14 points across tasks) demonstrates that foresight supervision contributes to enhanced performance in real-world robotic control settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

LiLa-WAM represents a contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing critical challenges related to computational efficiency and accessibility in world-action modeling.

One of its primary significances lies in demonstrating that **high performance in complex robotic manipulation tasks can be achieved with a lightweight architecture trainable on modest computational resources (a single 24GB GPU)**. This significantly lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners, making advanced world-action models more accessible for development, experimentation, and deployment in various settings. This efficiency contributes to the sustainability of research and development in robotics, reducing the need for extensive computational infrastructure.

The **unified design of the Foresight-Aware Action Expert**, which integrates future-state prediction and action generation into a single, end-to-end trainable stream, offers an alternative to the multi-stage training pipelines common in other latent-space WAMs. This architectural choice simplifies the training process and ensures that the compact latent space is jointly shaped by both predictive and generative objectives, promoting a tighter alignment with control requirements. The ablation studies confirm that explicit foresight supervision is a key contributor to performance, reinforcing the value of this integrated approach.

The introduction of the **Visual Transition Token (VTT)** is a notable development in task specification. By encoding tasks as language-free transition directions in visual feature space, the VTT provides a lightweight, efficient, and deployable alternative to traditional language instructions or goal-image conditioning. Its ability to concentrate attention on task-relevant objects without requiring explicit text or goal images at test time has the potential to simplify task definition and improve out-of-distribution generalization by focusing on the functional changes in the scene.

The **empirical validation across diverse simulation environments (RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO) and real-robot tasks** substantiates the practical applicability and robustness of LiLa-WAM. Its competitive performance against significantly larger models indicates that a focused, control-relevant architecture can be more effective than simply scaling up general-purpose pre-trained models.

The insights from attention analyses and action perturbation probes further inform the design of future robotic policies. They reveal a complementary division of labor between task-level cues (VTT) and action-focused attention, and confirm that the predicted future states are genuinely action-conditioned, highlighting the learned dynamics.

In summary, LiLa-WAM advances robot learning by proposing a resource-efficient yet highly capable world-action model. Its lightweight design, end-to-end training, and novel language-free task specification could influence the future trajectory of embodied AI research, promoting the development of more compact, efficient, and task-specific generalist robot policies capable of robust real-world manipulation.
