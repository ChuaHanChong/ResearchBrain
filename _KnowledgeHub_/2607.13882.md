# Learning Forward & Reverse Skills from a Single Unfinished Demonstration for Constrained Manipulation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework from TU Wien and DLR enables robots to learn both forward and reverse skills for contact-rich manipulation from a single, potentially unfinished demonstration, utilizing screw primitive modeling and admittance control. This approach demonstrated 100% success in forward and reverse tasks across various constrained scenarios using complete demonstrations.

## Problem

- Traditional Learning from Demonstration (LfD) methods often require numerous complete demonstrations, making data collection for complex, contact-rich tasks expensive and time-consuming.
- Existing LfD approaches struggle with constrained manipulation tasks involving precise force interactions and tight geometric tolerances, as they primarily focus on free-space trajectory reproduction.
- Current skill learning frameworks are predominantly unidirectional, lacking the ability to perform reverse task execution or learn effectively from incomplete or unfinished demonstrations.

## Method

- Decomposes a single human-guided demonstration into distinct non-contact (==DMP==-based) and contact phases, with contact motion segmented by a novel ==twist-direction-based segmentation== algorithm.
- Models each contact segment as a ==screw motion primitive==, representing the constrained behavior geometrically, and integrates wrench-aware ==6D admittance control== for robust execution.
- Supports reverse skill execution by reusing learned screw primitives with inverted parameters and applying them in reverse order, complemented by time-reversed ==DMPs== for free-space motion.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved a **100% success rate** (**20/20** trials) for both forward and reverse execution of contact-rich tasks using complete demonstrations, surpassing all baseline methods.
- It maintained high performance with unfinished demonstrations, achieving an **18/20 success rate** for both forward and reverse tasks by effectively extrapolating screw primitives.
- The novel twist-direction-based segmentation algorithm demonstrated superior performance in identifying contact phase transitions, with an F1 score of **0.98+-0.07** at **0.8s** tolerance, compared to existing segmentation techniques.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Geometric screw representations provide a robust and generalizable way to model constrained motions in contact-rich tasks, outperforming simple trajectory-based methods.
> - A twist-direction-based trajectory segmentation algorithm accurately identifies distinct contact phases, proving more reliable for short, constrained motions than existing segmentation methods.
> - Integrating explicit screw primitive modeling with interaction-aware admittance control enables robust skill execution and extrapolation beyond the demonstrated trajectory, even from unfinished data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges in robot manipulation, specifically within the paradigm of Learning from Demonstration (LfD). LfD aims to enable robots to acquire skills directly from human expert demonstrations, offering a method for robots to operate in unstructured environments. However, the paper identifies significant limitations of existing LfD approaches, particularly for contact-rich tasks such as insertion, fastening, and unlocking. These tasks are characterized by geometric constraints, intermittent contact, and precise force interactions, which pose difficulties for conventional LfD methods.

Many current LfD techniques, including those based on Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMP) and Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM), typically require multiple complete demonstrations to generalize effectively. These methods primarily focus on smooth trajectory reproduction in free-space manipulation and generally do not support learning skills from incomplete data or executing tasks in reverse. While recent vision-based and multimodal learning approaches show performance in manipulation, they often rely on extensive datasets or pretraining, which can be expensive and time-consuming to collect for complex contact-rich scenarios.

Classical control strategies, such as impedance or force control, can achieve robust physical interaction in constrained tasks. However, these methods usually require task-specific modeling or prior geometric knowledge, which limits their generalizability and efficiency when learning new skills.

Screw theory offers a geometric framework for representing rigid-body motion, which is suitable for constrained tasks. Existing screw-based methods, however, frequently focus on free-space motion or model an entire task as a single global screw motion, often failing to capture the multi-phase nature of contact-rich manipulation involving sequential geometric constraints.

Trajectory segmentation, crucial for decomposing demonstrations into reusable skills, often relies on kinematic heuristics or probabilistic models. These methods can be sensitive to noise, occlusions, and short-term fluctuations, making them less reliable for the short, highly constrained motions prevalent in contact-rich tasks where speed changes or dwell behaviors are not consistently indicative of true task transitions. Furthermore, the reversibility of continuous skills learned from a single demonstration is less explored, with existing work primarily focusing on higher-level abstractions like reversible robot programs or part-level assembly sequences.

This research aims to bridge these gaps by developing a data-efficient, unified framework that can learn both forward and reverse skills from a single, potentially unfinished demonstration, specifically designed to handle the geometric constraints and force interactions inherent in contact-rich manipulation. It integrates geometric screw representations with interaction-aware execution to provide a robust and generalizable solution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified one-shot learning framework for constrained, contact-rich robot manipulation tasks. This framework is designed to overcome several limitations observed in existing Learning from Demonstration (LfD) methodologies.

The motivation stems from the inherent difficulties of applying LfD to real-world, contact-rich tasks like peg insertion, battery insertion, lock opening, and screw driving. These tasks involve tight geometric tolerances, intermittent contact, and significant force interactions, which are challenging for traditional LfD approaches.

Specifically, the authors identify and aim to address the following key problems:
- **Requirement for Multiple Complete Demonstrations:** Most LfD methods necessitate numerous complete demonstrations to generalize effectively and capture task variability. Collecting such data for contact-rich tasks is often expensive, time-consuming, or physically demanding for human demonstrators. The research is motivated by the need for a framework that can learn effectively from a *single demonstration*.
- **Robustness to Unfinished Demonstrations:** In practical scenarios, demonstrations might be unfinished due to safety considerations, physical limitations of the demonstrator, or the repetitive nature of sub-motions. Existing methods typically assume complete demonstrations, limiting their applicability in these situations. A core motivation is to enable learning from a *potentially unfinished demonstration*.
- **Lack of Reverse Skill Execution:** Current skill learning frameworks are predominantly directional, meaning a robot typically cannot perform the reverse of a learned skill (e.g., unlocking from a demonstration of locking) without additional, explicit demonstrations for the reverse task. The paper is motivated to enable *both forward and reverse skill execution* from a single demonstration, without requiring new learning data for the reverse task.
- **Inadequacy of Trajectory-Based Methods for Contact-Rich Tasks:** While methods like Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMP) are effective for free-space motion, they struggle to capture the structured geometric behaviors and strong constraints inherent in contact-rich manipulation.
- **Limitations of Classical Control Approaches:** Traditional force or impedance control methods can handle interaction, but they usually require task-specific modeling or prior geometric knowledge, which limits their generalizability across diverse tasks.
- **Geometric Understanding:** The authors observe that many contact-rich manipulation tasks can be naturally decomposed into sequences of constrained motions exhibiting consistent "screw-like" motion behavior (e.g., insertion, rotation, coupled translation-rotation). This insight motivates developing a method that explicitly leverages these invariant geometric structures to enable generalization beyond the demonstrated trajectory length.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to provide a more practical, data-efficient, and versatile LfD solution for complex robot manipulation in constrained environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework is a unified one-shot learning approach designed for constrained, contact-rich manipulation tasks. It decomposes a single human-guided demonstration into distinct non-contact and contact phases, applying different learning and execution strategies to each.

**A. Overview of the Learning and Execution Pipeline**
A human demonstration, typically a kinesthetic teaching session, provides an end-effector trajectory. This full trajectory is initially encoded using a Dynamic Movement Primitive (DMP). However, the DMP is only executed during the non-contact phase. Contact is detected via wrench feedback, at which point the system transitions to the contact phase.

The contact phase of the demonstrated trajectory is then segmented using a novel twist-direction-based method. Each identified segment is subsequently modeled as a screw motion primitive, creating a geometric representation of the constrained behavior.

At the onset of contact, the system assesses if local alignment is necessary. This involves comparing the short-window motion directions before and after contact detection. If directions are consistent but significant contact force is present, indicating misalignment, a spiral search procedure is initiated to re-align the end-effector.

Following this, the learned screw primitives are executed sequentially. The execution incorporates wrench-aware admittance control, enabling task completion even if it extends beyond the original demonstrated trajectory length. For unfinished demonstrations, the method extrapolates observed primitives, but it requires that task-critical contact phases are at least partially observed.

For **reverse execution**, the framework reuses the learned screw primitives. Their parameters are automatically edited (e.g., reversing twist direction), and they are executed in reverse order under the same admittance control. Transition from contact to free space during reverse execution is determined by wrench-based contact release detection. The subsequent free-space motion is then reproduced by a DMP learned from the time-reversed forward demonstration trajectory.

**B. Twist-direction-based Trajectory Segmentation**
This algorithm segments the contact-phase trajectory into a sequence of motion segments, each characterized by a consistent twist direction. It aims to be robust to noise and short contact motions by focusing purely on geometric motion directions.
1. **Direction-only Twist Features:** The demonstrated pose trajectory is smoothed in SE(3). For each step, the relative motion ∆Tᵢ is computed, and its logarithmic map provides the twist ξ̂ᵢ∆θᵢ. From this, a 6D direction feature fᵢ = (dᵨᵢ, dᵥᵢ) is extracted, representing the rotation axis direction and translation direction, respectively, in the local frame. Negligible rotations or translations are set to zero.
2. **Block-wise Dominant Direction Estimation:** The sequence of direction features {fᵢ} is partitioned into fixed-length blocks. Within each block, PCA is used to estimate the dominant rotation axis μᵨ and translation direction μᵥ, with sign ambiguities resolved by aligning with block endpoint directions.
3. **Block Scanning and Boundary Detection:** Blocks are processed sequentially. A candidate boundary is detected if the current block's dominant directions (μᵨⱼ, μᵥⱼ) deviate significantly (below thresholds τᵨ or τᵥ in cosine similarity) from the dominant directions of the current segment (gᵨ, gᵥ). To prevent false positives, an additional condition requires that the subsequent block's directions (μᵨⱼ₊₁, μᵥⱼ₊₁) also do not align with the current segment's directions.
4. **In-block Boundary Refinement:** When a transition block is identified, a sample-level changepoint within the block is refined by minimizing a direction-consistency cost. This cost measures how well samples before a candidate split point align with the current segment's direction and how well samples after it align with the new segment's direction.
5. **Global Geometric Refinement:** To ensure global consistency and merge spurious short segments, adjacent segments are merged if their dominant rotation and translation directions are sufficiently similar (above thresholds τᵨ and τᵥ).
6. **Short-motion (Dwell) Cleanup:** Segments with negligible accumulated translation and rotation are identified and merged into adjacent segments, as they do not represent meaningful contact-mode transitions.

**C. Screw Primitive Learning**
After segmentation, each contact-motion segment is modeled as a constant screw motion primitive.
1. **Screw Primitive Parameterization:** For a segment {Tᵢ}, relative poses Tᵣₑₗᵢ = T₀⁻¹Tᵢ are used. The objective is to estimate a consistent twist ξ and scalar progress variables θᵢ such that Tᵣₑₗᵢ ≈ exp(ξ̂θᵢ). The twist ξ is parameterized by screw coordinates (ŝ, q, h).
2. **Screw Parameters Optimization:** The screw primitive is estimated by minimizing a weighted least-squares problem in the Lie algebra se(3). Residuals are defined as the logarithm of the pose discrepancy. A reduced-parameter strategy is employed for computational efficiency, where progress variables θᵢ are initialized as a monotone sequence scaled by a global factor `s`, reducing the optimization variables to (ŝ, q, h, s). Huber loss is used for robustness.
3. **Pure Translation Segments:** If a segment has negligible accumulated rotation, it is treated as pure translation. A direction d̂ is estimated via PCA, and the twist is ξ = (0, d̂), with progress θᵢ corresponding to displacement.
4. **Primitive Representation:** Each learned segment is represented as a screw primitive πₙ = {ŝₙ, qₙ, hₙ, θᵉⁿᵈₙ}, where θᵉⁿᵈₙ is used to initialize execution speed.

**D. Execution with Admittance-Guided Screw Primitives**
During execution, each screw primitive πₙ defines a constrained motion parameterized by a progress variable θ.
1. **Nominal Pose Generation:** A desired pose Tᵈᵉˢ(θ) = T₀ exp(ξ̂ₙθ) is generated at each control cycle.
2. **6D Admittance Pose Correction:** The nominal pose is modulated online using a 6D admittance control law. A small pose correction ∆xₑₑ is generated from the measured wrench wₑₑ through standard admittance dynamics (M∆ẍₑₑ + B∆ẋₑₑ + K∆xₑₑ = wₑₑ).
3. **1D Admittance Speed Regulation:** Wrench feedback is also used to regulate execution speed along the screw axis. The measured wrench is decomposed into axial and perpendicular components relative to the screw axis (transformed into the end-effector frame). A scalar load `L` is computed from these components, with axial terms receiving higher weights. This load `L` then regulates the progress velocity ˙θ through a 1D admittance model (Mᵨ˙vᵨ + Bᵨvᵨ = −L, ˙θ = ˙θ₀ + vᵨ), causing speed to decrease as contact resistance increases.
4. **Termination:** A screw primitive is considered complete when the effective progress velocity remains below a predefined threshold for a sustained duration.

**E. Reverse Skill Learning and Execution**
The framework natively supports reverse task execution.
1. **Primitive Reversal:** The learned screw primitives {πₙ} are reused. For reverse execution, each primitive is edited (˜πₙ) by reversing its twist parameters (ω, v) → (−ω, −v).
2. **Execution Order:** The reversed primitives are executed in reverse order: {˜πN..., ˜π1}.
3. **Contact Release Detection:** During reverse execution, particularly when moving out of contact, an active wrench-based probing test is used to detect contact release. A small circular dither motion orthogonal to the screw axis is executed, and the load is monitored. If the median absolute load falls below a threshold, contact is considered released, and the system transitions to the next primitive or the free-space phase.
4. **Free-space Motion:** The remaining free-space motion after contact release is reproduced by a DMP learned from the time-reversed trajectory of the forward demonstration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed framework was evaluated through a series of experiments on a Franka Emika Panda robot with a wrist-mounted force/torque sensor. Kinesthetic teaching was used to provide single demonstrations for each task. AprilTags were used only for initial object pose estimation, with no visual feedback during execution. Wrench safety limits were enforced. The evaluation involved four methods across five randomized trials per task for both forward and reverse execution, using both complete and unfinished demonstrations, totaling 80 trials per method. Segmentation parameters were fixed across tasks, while safety thresholds and scalar load weights were task-dependent.

**A. Tasks and Success Criteria**
Four contact-rich manipulation tasks were used:
1. **Peg-in-hole insertion:** Inserting a rectangular peg into a hole (1mm clearance). Forward success: insertion depth > 2.3cm. Reverse success: complete extraction without exceeding wrench limits.
2. **Battery insertion:** Three sequential contact phases (sliding, tilting, seating). Forward success: ≥ 75% of battery surface inside bed. Reverse success: complete extraction.
3. **Lock opening:** Inserting and rotating a key to fully open a lock (<0.5mm clearance). Forward success: lock fully opened. Reverse success: complete key extraction.
4. **Screw driving:** Rotating a partially threaded screw until tight. Regrasping was handled by stitching demonstration segments and an execution policy. Forward success: reach tightening point and stop. Reverse success: back out screw until it loses self-supporting stability. Unfinished demonstrations were generated by truncating the contact phase of complete demonstrations.

**B. Baselines**
Three one-shot learning baselines were used for comparison:
1. **DMP only:** The entire demonstration trajectory (non-contact and contact) was reproduced using a single DMP. Contact detection, spiral search, and wrench safety limits were identical to the proposed method.
2. **DMP + admittance:** Similar to DMP only, but with 6D Cartesian admittance correction during execution.
3. **DMP + twist + admittance:** Non-contact phases used DMP. Contact phases were segmented by the proposed method, but each segment was represented by a constant twist derived from start-end displacement. Execution employed the same admittance correction and speed regulation as the proposed method. For reverse tasks, DMP-based baselines generally did not attempt contact-phase extraction unless explicitly enabled.

**C. Task Performance with Complete Demonstrations (Table I)**
- **Proposed Method (Ours):** Achieved 20/20 success rates for both forward and reverse tasks.
- **DMP only:** Demonstrated 15/20 success forward and 14/20 reverse. It performed well in insertion-dominant tasks but struggled with screw driving, where success depended on matching specific demonstrated thread lengths.
- **DMP + Admittance:** Achieved 12/20 success forward and 15/20 reverse. While improving some contact interactions, it failed in highly constrained tasks like lock opening and screw driving, where admittance-induced deviations could prevent the robot from achieving the required geometric configurations for task progression.
- **DMP + Twist + Admittance:** Showed 15/20 success forward and 10/20 reverse. It was competitive in peg and battery tasks but failed in screw driving and reverse lock opening because simple start-end twists did not adequately capture accumulated rotational structures or the precise geometric requirements for reverse extraction.
- **Conclusion:** These results indicate the importance of explicit screw primitive fitting for maintaining geometric consistency in constrained manipulation, which is crucial for high success rates.

**D. Task Performance with Unfinished Demonstrations (Table II)**
- **Proposed Method (Ours):** Achieved 18/20 success in both forward and reverse settings. Failures occurred only in screw driving, attributed mainly to initial pose errors and pitch deviations from trajectory stitching accumulating over multiple rotations, leading to axis misalignment. The method's ability to extrapolate motion beyond the demonstrated length was critical.
- **DMP only & DMP + Admittance:** Both achieved 2/20 success forward and 15/20 reverse. These methods terminated at the demonstrated endpoint, lacking the mechanism to extend forward contact motion, leading to failures in tasks requiring completion beyond the demonstration.
- **DMP + Twist + Admittance:** Achieved 14/20 success forward and 10/20 reverse. While it could extend motion, it degraded in screw driving and reverse tasks due to the same geometric inconsistencies observed with complete demonstrations.
- **Conclusion:** The findings demonstrate that screw primitive modeling combined with interaction-aware control enables reliable task completion even from partial demonstrations under geometric constraints.

**E. Segmentation Evaluation (Table III)**
The proposed twist-direction segmentation algorithm was compared against Human-Guided Screw Planning (HGS), Kernel Temporal Segmentation (KTS), and Automatic Waypoint Extraction (AWE).
- **Metrics:** Boundary F1 score at tolerances τ ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 0.8}s, Variation of Information (VI), and segment count error (Cnt.). Dwell cleanup was applied to all methods.
- **Proposed Method (Ours):** Achieved an overall better performance (e.g., F1@0.8 of 0.98±0.07, VI of 0.29±0.32). Its approach of detecting transitions based on twist-direction consistency, considering both local changes and global coherence, yielded more stable boundaries for short, highly constrained motions.
- **AWE:** Performed lowest, relying on waypoint reconstruction error, which made it brittle in rotation-dominant scenarios.
- **HGS:** Showed the lowest segment count error but was less stable in boundary detection.
- **KTS:** Achieved the best performance among the baselines but still underperformed the proposed method.
- **Conclusion:** The twist-direction-based segmentation method provides more stable and accurate segmentation for contact-rich tasks compared to the baselines.

**F. Generalization to Novel Objects (Fig. 6)**
The learned screw primitives were applied to novel objects with different dimensions and target positions for each task. All four tasks were successfully completed.
- **Conclusion:** This indicates that the proposed method effectively captures task-consistent motion structures and exhibits transferability across objects with similar contact topology, provided the target position is given and control is handled by admittance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of Learning from Demonstration (LfD) for robot manipulation, particularly for tasks involving contact and geometric constraints. The primary contributions and their implications are:

**Significance:**
- **One-Shot Learning for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The framework demonstrates the feasibility of learning complex, contact-rich manipulation skills from a *single human demonstration*. This significantly reduces the data collection burden, which is often a bottleneck in robot learning, especially for safety-critical or tedious contact tasks.
- **Robustness to Incomplete Data:** By enabling learning from *potentially unfinished demonstrations*, the method offers practical utility in scenarios where complete demonstrations are difficult or impossible to obtain. The ability to extrapolate observed primitives allows for task completion beyond the demonstrated trajectory length.
- **Bidirectional Skill Execution:** The framework intrinsically supports both *forward and reverse execution* of learned skills without requiring additional demonstrations for the reverse task. This enhances robot versatility and simplifies the programming of reversible operations, such as assembly and disassembly.
- **Geometry-Driven Contact Handling:** The core innovation lies in decomposing contact-rich motion into sequences of screw primitives, which are robust geometric representations. This approach explicitly addresses the structured geometric behaviors found in tasks like insertion and rotation, which are poorly captured by generic trajectory-based methods.
- **Interaction-Aware Execution:** The integration of 6D admittance for pose correction and 1D admittance for speed regulation allows the robot to adapt to environmental uncertainties and robustly handle force interactions during execution, extending its capabilities beyond simple trajectory following.
- **Improved Segmentation:** The proposed twist-direction-based segmentation algorithm provides a robust and task-agnostic method for decomposing contact motion into meaningful screw primitives, outperforming existing segmentation techniques in this context.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Increased Robot Autonomy:** The ability to learn complex, constrained skills efficiently from minimal data makes robots more autonomous and adaptable to diverse real-world environments, including manufacturing, service robotics, and home assistance.
- **Simplified Robot Programming:** The framework reduces the need for manual, task-specific modeling or extensive programming, making robot deployment more accessible to non-experts.
- **Enhanced Robot Capabilities:** The explicit handling of geometric constraints and force interactions allows robots to perform delicate and precise manipulation tasks that were previously challenging.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The method provides a robust foundation for future research in areas such as learning more complex contact topologies, integrating visual feedback, optimizing execution speed, and adapting to more significant variations in object properties.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
- **Execution Speed Optimization:** The current framework's execution speed was not optimized, suggesting potential for improvements through faster alignment mechanisms and primitive-speed selection strategies.
- **Axis/Pitch Misalignment Accumulation:** In tasks like screw driving with regrasping, sustained Cartesian admittance offsets could lead to the accumulation of axis or pitch misalignment. Future work aims to explore screw-aware admittance control and online primitive adaptation to mitigate this.
- **Generalization Scope:** The current generalization primarily applies to objects with similar contact topology. Expanding this to broader variations in object properties or contact types represents an area for future investigation.

Overall, this work demonstrates a practical and data-efficient solution for challenging constrained robot manipulation tasks by effectively integrating geometric screw representations with interaction-aware execution.
