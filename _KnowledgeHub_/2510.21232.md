# How Hard is it to Confuse a World Model?

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Univ. Lille, Inria, and CNRS developed a method to formally identify and quantify the "suboptimality cost" of confusing instances for neural network world models in reinforcement learning. Their adversarial training approach revealed that a model's resistance to confusion increases directly with its training quality, requiring significantly more statistical perturbation to induce different optimal policies in well-trained models.

## Problem

- Model-based reinforcement learning (RL) faces challenges in effectively guiding exploration due to uncertainty in learned dynamics models.
- Existing deep RL methods often rely on heuristic uncertainty quantification lacking a direct theoretical connection to optimal exploration principles.
- The practical construction of theoretically grounded "confusing instances" for general Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with learned neural network models remains an open and computationally difficult problem.

## Method

- The research formally defines a "confusing instance" as an alternative model statistically close to a reference model that inverts the performance ranking of an optimal and a suboptimal policy.
- It introduces the "suboptimality cost" as the minimal statistical evidence (KL divergence) required to distinguish the true model from such a confusing instance.
- A tractable optimization procedure, based on adversarial training with Lagrangian relaxation, is proposed to empirically identify these confusing instances in differentiable probabilistic world models.

## Results

- Under-trained models (100 epochs) were confused rapidly, with "time to first constraint satisfaction" averaging 165 iterations, while well-trained models (500 epochs) required 1506 iterations.
- The suboptimality cost (K) was observed to increase monotonically with model quality, indicating that better-trained models constrain the space of plausible, yet behaviorally distinct, alternatives more effectively.
- Visualizations demonstrated that confusing instances localized perturbations to specific environmental features (e.g., creating shortcuts through walls), leaving most of the state space's dynamics unchanged.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A direct correlation exists between the training quality of a world model and its resistance to being confused, with well-trained models exhibiting greater resistance.
> - Confusing instances achieve policy ranking inversion through minimal, targeted statistical perturbations, often altering decision-critical dynamics (e.g., making walls permeable) while maintaining overall statistical similarity.
> - The suboptimality cost offers a theoretically grounded metric for decision-relevant uncertainty, quantifying how difficult it is to distinguish between competing optimal behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Model-based reinforcement learning (RL) aims to improve sample efficiency by learning dynamics models of an environment, which can then be used for planning and simulation. A persistent challenge in this field is addressing uncertainty in these learned world models to guide effective exploration. Existing deep RL methods frequently employ heuristic approaches for uncertainty quantification, such as measuring ensemble disagreement, incorporating prediction error bonuses, or utilizing variational inference in Bayesian neural networks. While these heuristics have demonstrated empirical success, they often lack a direct theoretical connection to the principles of optimal exploration.

In contrast, the domain of multi-armed bandits (MABs) provides a more precise theoretical framework for characterizing exploration difficulty through the concept of regret lower bounds. Optimal algorithms in MABs are required to gather sufficient statistical evidence to differentiate the true environment from "confusing instances"—plausible alternative models that are statistically similar but necessitate different optimal actions. Exploration strategies that explicitly construct and resolve such confusing instances have been shown to achieve asymptotically optimal regret in various MAB settings.

However, the practical construction of these confusing instances, particularly for general Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with continuous or high-dimensional state-action spaces and learned neural network models, remains an open problem. The search for such instances in general MDPs is known to be NP-hard. This research addresses this gap by investigating the empirical feasibility of identifying confusing instances for probabilistic neural network world models, thus attempting to bridge theoretical insights from MABs with practical challenges in deep model-based RL. The work aims to develop a theoretically grounded measure of uncertainty for exploration beyond current heuristic approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to formalize and empirically investigate the problem of identifying the "most confusing instance" for neural network world models in reinforcement learning. This involves finding an alternative model that is statistically closest to a learned reference model while inducing a different optimal policy.

The specific objectives of the paper are:
- To formally define a "confusing instance" and the "suboptimality cost" in the context of learned world models. A confusing instance is characterized as an alternative model that is statistically close to a reference model but causes an inversion in the performance ranking of an optimal and a suboptimal policy. The suboptimality cost quantifies the minimal statistical evidence (measured by KL divergence) required to distinguish the true model from such a confusing instance.
- To propose a tractable optimization procedure, specifically an adversarial training method based on Lagrangian relaxation, for identifying these confusing instances in differentiable probabilistic world models.
- To conduct an empirical study across world models of varying quality (trained for different durations) to assess how model quality affects the ease with which these models can be confused.
- To analyze the relationship between the derived suboptimality cost and model uncertainty, exploring its potential as a theoretically-grounded metric for informing exploration strategies in deep model-based RL.

The motivation for pursuing these objectives stems from several points:
- **Addressing limitations of heuristic exploration:** Current deep RL exploration strategies often rely on heuristic uncertainty quantification, which lacks direct theoretical grounding in the requirements for optimal exploration. The paper seeks to introduce a principled measure of decision-relevant uncertainty.
- **Bridging theory and practice:** The concept of confusing instances is central to establishing regret lower bounds in theoretical RL settings like MABs, but its application to complex, high-dimensional learned world models has been largely unexplored due to computational challenges. This work aims to bridge this theoretical understanding with practical deep RL applications.
- **Quantifying exploration difficulty:** The suboptimality cost, defined as the minimal statistical evidence needed to distinguish between competing optimal policies, offers a quantitative measure of how "confusing" a model is. A low cost suggests the model is easily misled, requiring more data for robust decision-making, while a high cost indicates greater reliability.
- **Informing future exploration strategies:** By understanding "how hard it is to confuse a world model," the authors aim to lay the groundwork for developing new exploration strategies that specifically target critical uncertainties identified by the suboptimality cost. This could lead to more efficient and theoretically sound data collection in complex environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formalizes the search for the most confusing instance as a constrained optimization problem and proposes an adversarial training procedure to solve it empirically for neural network world models.

**Problem Formalization:**
The authors consider episodic reinforcement learning in continuous state and action spaces with a fixed horizon. A learned world model $M_{\theta}$ approximates environment dynamics through probabilistic transitions $P_{\theta}(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t)$.
- **Confusing Instance (Definition 1):** A model $\hat{M} = M_{\tilde{\theta}}$ is defined as a confusing instance if it is statistically close to the reference model $M_{\theta}$ (measured by a small expected log-likelihood ratio, $KL^{\pi^*_T}(M_{\theta}||M_{\tilde{\theta}})$) and inverts the policy ranking, meaning $V_{\pi^*}(s_0; \hat{M}) < V_{\pi_{sub}}(s_0; \hat{M})$, where $\pi^*$ is optimal and $\pi_{sub}$ is suboptimal under $M_{\theta}$.
- **Suboptimality Cost (Definition 2):** The suboptimality cost $K(s_0, \pi^*, \pi_{sub})$ is the infimum of KL divergences over all confusing instances that satisfy the policy ranking inversion constraint. This cost quantifies the minimal statistical evidence required to distinguish the policies.

**Optimization Procedure:**
Finding the suboptimality cost involves a challenging constrained optimization problem, as it requires expectations over stochastic trajectories and value functions. The authors employ Lagrangian relaxation to address this:
$L(\tilde{\theta}, \lambda) = KL^{\pi^*_T}(M_{\theta}||M_{\tilde{\theta}}) + \lambda \max(0, V_{\pi^*}(M_{\tilde{\theta}}) - V_{\pi_{sub}}(M_{\tilde{\theta}}))$
The optimization alternates between primal updates for the model parameters $\tilde{\theta}$ (via gradient descent on the Lagrangian) and dual updates for the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$. The multiplier $\lambda$ increases when the constraint (policy ranking inversion) is violated, penalizing the objective. When the constraint is satisfied, the current KL divergence is recorded as a candidate for $K$. The infimum of these recorded KL values represents the suboptimality cost.

**World Model Architecture and Training:**
- The experiments utilize variational autoencoder (VAE) based world models. These models consist of an encoder ($\phi$) mapping states to latent codes, a dynamics model ($\psi$) predicting the next latent state, and a decoder ($\xi$) reconstructing states from latent codes.
- The models are implemented using two-layer MLPs with tanh activations. The encoder maps observations in $\mathbb{R}^4$ to latent codes in $\mathbb{R}^{d_z}$ ($d_z \in \{32, 64\}$). The dynamics model outputs Gaussian parameters for the next latent state. The decoder reconstructs state differences.
- Models are trained on 50,000 transitions collected through random exploration in a U-shaped maze. The training loss combines reconstruction error and KL regularization for the VAE.
- Training occurs over 500 epochs, with checkpoints saved at intervals (100, 200, 300, 400, 500 epochs) to provide a spectrum of model qualities, from under-trained to well-trained.

**Confusing Instance Search Implementation:**
- To focus on dynamical uncertainty, the encoder ($\phi$) and decoder ($\xi$) are frozen during the confusing instance search; only the latent dynamics model ($\psi$) is perturbed.
- For Gaussian latent dynamics, the pointwise KL divergence has a closed-form solution, which is averaged along trajectories to approximate the trajectory-level KL divergence.
- The optimization runs for 2500 iterations, using 128 trajectory samples per iteration. Learning rates for dynamics parameters are scheduled linearly, and gradient clipping is applied. The Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ is initialized and updated with a fixed step size. Algorithm 1 outlines the complete procedure.

**Task and Policy Construction:**
- The evaluation environment is a 5x5 U-shaped maze with continuous state representation (2D position and velocity) and a goal-based reward (negative Manhattan distance).
- Two policies are constructed:
 - **Optimal policy ($\pi^*$):** Computed via Breadth-First Search (BFS) on the original discretized U-Maze, following the shortest path.
 - **Suboptimal policy ($\pi_{sub}$):** Computed via BFS on a *modified* maze where a wall is repositioned to create an alternative, shorter path in the modified maze. When executed in the *original* U-Maze, this policy becomes suboptimal as it attempts to pass through a wall, resulting in a longer route.
- Both policies incorporate Gaussian noise to introduce stochasticity in rollouts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical investigation yielded several findings concerning the relationship between world model quality and its resistance to confusion:

**Model Quality and Confusion Resistance:**
- The study observed a direct correlation between the training quality of the world model and its resistance to confusion. Under-trained models (e.g., those from epochs 100-200) were found to be easily confused; the constraint requiring policy ranking inversion was satisfied rapidly during the adversarial optimization.
- In contrast, well-trained models (e.g., from epochs 300-500) exhibited greater resistance. For these models, the optimizer had to explore significantly higher KL divergence values and required more iterations to identify a confusing instance that satisfied the policy inversion constraint.
- This relationship was quantified by the "time to first constraint satisfaction," which increased monotonically with model quality, ranging from 165 iterations for a model trained for 100 epochs to 1506 iterations for a model trained for 500 epochs. The observed growth appeared sublinear or logarithmic, suggesting that as models improve, the marginal increase in resistance diminishes. This pattern aligns with theoretical expectations of efficient exploration strategies that accumulate evidence at diminishing rates. The suboptimality cost $K$ was found to increase monotonically with model quality, indicating that better-trained models constrain the space of statistically plausible, yet behaviorally distinct, alternatives more effectively.

**Statistical Closeness of Confusing Instances:**
- Despite inducing a different optimal behavior, the identified confusing instances remained statistically close to their reference models. Visualizations of state visitation densities under a random policy showed overall similar patterns between the reference and confused models.
- Analysis of the log-density ratio revealed that the mechanism of confusion was localized. Blue regions in the log-density ratio map indicated areas where the confused model assigned a higher visitation probability. These regions frequently corresponded to wall locations, suggesting that the confused model effectively "removed" or altered the dynamics of these walls, creating shortcuts that made the originally suboptimal policy viable.
- Conversely, red regions (lower probability in the confused model) and predominantly white regions (preserved statistical similarity) indicated that most of the state space remained largely unaffected. This suggests that the confusing instance achieved behavioral change through minimal, targeted perturbations, exploiting the reference model's uncertainty specifically in decision-critical areas such as wall dynamics, while maintaining consistent predictions elsewhere.

**Dynamics Evolution During Search:**
- Visualizations of trajectory rollouts during the confusing instance search reinforced the quantitative findings. At the beginning of the search (iteration 0), the suboptimal policy ($\pi_{sub}$) failed to navigate the maze effectively, often becoming trapped by walls.
- As the optimization progressed, the dynamics of the confusing model candidate ($M_{\tilde{\theta}}$) were perturbed. For under-trained models (e.g., epoch 100), successful suboptimal trajectories (reaching the goal through the "shortcut" wall) appeared relatively early in the optimization, by iteration 300.
- In contrast, for well-trained models (e.g., epoch 500), the suboptimal policy remained trapped for a significantly longer duration, with successful trajectories only emerging after over 1500 iterations. This directly illustrated the increased resistance to confusion in higher-quality models, confirming the relationship between model quality and suboptimality cost.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning by offering a theoretically-grounded perspective on model uncertainty and its implications for exploration.

- **Principled Uncertainty Measure:** The formalization and empirical validation of "suboptimality cost" (K) as a measure of decision-relevant uncertainty provide a principled alternative to heuristic uncertainty quantification methods. This cost directly quantifies the minimal statistical evidence required to distinguish between policies, aligning with information-theoretic exploration requirements.
- **Insights into Exploration Difficulty:** The finding that suboptimality cost increases with model quality offers a quantifiable metric for how "hard" it is to confuse a world model. This insight suggests that better-trained models are inherently more resistant to plausible alternative explanations that would necessitate different optimal behaviors, thus potentially requiring less exploration in those well-understood regions.
- **Informing Future Exploration Strategies:** The framework could pave the way for novel, theoretically-informed exploration strategies in deep model-based RL. Agents could potentially use the suboptimality cost to actively identify states or regions where the current model is most easily confused (i.e., where K is low). Prioritizing data collection in these regions could lead to more efficient and targeted learning, addressing critical uncertainties directly rather than relying on generalized error metrics.
- **Bridging Theory and Practice:** The work attempts to bridge a gap between theoretical RL, where concepts like confusing instances are fundamental for regret lower bounds, and practical deep RL, which has traditionally struggled to implement such theoretical constructs for complex, learned neural models.
- **Directions for Future Research:** The paper identifies several important avenues for future work, including integrating suboptimality cost into active exploration algorithms during planning, assessing the framework's scalability across different neural architectures and more complex environments, and extending the concept to partially observable environments. Addressing these questions could significantly advance the development of principled and efficient exploration strategies for complex sequential decision-making systems.
