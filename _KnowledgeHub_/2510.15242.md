# Dual-Weighted Reinforcement Learning for Generative Preference Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> Dual-Weighted Reinforcement Learning (DWRL) enables Generative Preference Models (GPMs) to integrate Chain-of-Thought reasoning into human preference tasks by preserving Bradley-Terry inductive bias. This approach, evaluated on Qwen2.5 and Llama3 models, consistently surpassed existing GPMs and standard preference models with up to a 9.1% improvement in accuracy on math reasoning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning is limited to tasks with objective, verifiable answers, struggling with subjective human preferences.
- Existing Generative Preference Models (GPMs) often underperformed simpler scalar preference models by failing to incorporate the crucial inductive bias of preference data when integrating CoT.
- Prior GPMs required costly supervised thought data for training and faced fragility due to strict output formatting for preference scores, limiting practical applicability.

## Method

- Introduced a ==dialog-based GPM architecture== that separates latent thought generation from final preference scoring, deriving scores from designated token logits to avoid formatting fragility.
- Developed ==Dual-Weighted Reinforcement Learning (DWRL)==, an RL algorithm that approximates the Bradley-Terry loss gradient using two complementary weighting terms: an ==instance-wise misalignment weight== and self-normalized conditional preference scores.
- Employed an alternating optimization strategy to update preference scoring and thought generation components, ensuring stable training by emphasizing misaligned preference pairs.

## Results

- DWRL consistently outperformed all baselines across Helpfulness & Harmlessness, Instruction Following, and Math Reasoning tasks, achieving accuracy gains of up to **4.8%**, **2.7%**, and **9.1%** respectively.
- Prior GRPO-based GPMs performed significantly worse than simple Bradley-Terry models, achieving near-random accuracy (**50-53%**) on Math Reasoning compared to BT's **76-84%**.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of the instance-wise misalignment weight; its removal led to substantial performance drops (e.g., from **70.5%** to **59.1%** on Helpfulness & Harmlessness).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly incorporating the Bradley-Terry inductive bias through an instance-wise misalignment weight is critical for effectively training GPMs, as naive RL applications discard this bias.
> - GPMs can learn to generate genuinely informative thoughts that enhance preference prediction accuracy, rather than merely leveraging pre-existing LLM reasoning capabilities.
> - Pointwise preference modeling demonstrates greater robustness for complex, reasoning-intensive tasks compared to pairwise GPMs, which can be less generalizable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the rapidly evolving field of large language models (LLMs), particularly concerning their reasoning capabilities and training methodologies. Recent advancements have demonstrated the efficacy of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) in scaling Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning for LLMs. This approach has proven successful in domains such as mathematics and coding, where the correctness of a generated answer can be automatically verified.

However, a significant challenge in the broader research landscape involves extending these RL-based CoT training paradigms to tasks where direct verification of an answer is difficult or impossible. In such "non-verifiable" tasks, supervision is typically provided through human preferences, often in the form of pairwise comparisons between candidate responses.

Traditional preference modeling, exemplified by the Bradley–Terry (BT) model, provides a statistically grounded framework for predicting human preferences by learning a scalar scoring function for responses. While robust, these traditional models operate on deterministic scores and do not intrinsically support the integration of explicit CoT reasoning generated by LLMs.

To address this, recent research has explored "generative preference models" (GPMs), which aim to augment scalar preference prediction with explicit CoT reasoning. These GPMs reframe preference modeling as a text generation task, where an LLM first generates an intermediate reasoning trace (CoT) and then produces a preference judgment. However, existing GPMs largely rely on heuristic applications of RL objectives designed for verifiable rewards. These methods often treat preference supervision as a simple binary reward signal (ee.g., 1 for preferred, 0 for less preferred) and apply standard policy gradient techniques. This heuristic approach has been observed to lead to unstable performance and, in some cases, suboptimal results compared to statistically simpler BT models.

This paper identifies a fundamental mismatch between the probabilistic nature of preference data and the heuristic RL objectives used in existing GPMs. It aims to bridge this gap by developing a statistically principled method that combines the reliability of the Bradley–Terry maximum-likelihood objective with the generative capabilities of RL, specifically accounting for CoT as a latent variable. By doing so, the work seeks to enable more stable and effective training of LLMs for preference-based tasks that require complex reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current approaches to training Large Language Models (LLMs) for non-verifiable tasks where human preferences serve as the primary supervision signal.

**Motivation:**

1. **Scaling CoT to Non-Verifiable Tasks:** Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has been effective in scaling Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning for tasks with clear, verifiable answers (e.g., mathematical problem-solving). However, extending this success to tasks lacking such verifiable ground truth, where supervision is based on subjective human preferences, remains an open problem.
2. **Limitations of Traditional Preference Models:** The Bradley–Terry (BT) model provides a statistically sound framework for pairwise preference modeling by learning a scalar score for each response. However, its reliance on a single scalar output limits its capacity to leverage the rich, sequential reasoning capabilities inherent in modern LLMs and the benefits observed in test-time scaling methods that involve explicit reasoning.
3. **Deficiencies of Heuristic Generative Preference Models (GPMs):** Recent efforts to incorporate CoT reasoning into preference modeling (Generative Preference Models or GPMs) often reframe the task as a text generation problem and apply standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) objectives. These heuristic approaches typically treat human preference signals as direct, verifiable rewards. This simplification disregards the probabilistic nature of preference data and the fact that CoT sequences are unobserved latent variables in the judgment process. Consequently, these methods have exhibited unstable performance and have frequently been outperformed by more statistically grounded, albeit less expressive, BT models. The paper specifically notes that existing GPMs optimize heuristic objectives not explicitly grounded in a preference likelihood, leading to indirect and potentially inefficient optimization of CoT reasoning.

Based on these motivations, the authors established the following objectives:

1. **Formulate a Principled BT Extension for CoT:** To introduce a novel extension of the Bradley–Terry preference model that formally integrates Chain-of-Thought (CoT) token sequences as latent variables within the preference likelihood function. This formulation recognizes that the reasoning process mediates the final preference judgment and is not directly observed.
2. **Derive a Consistent Gradient Estimator:** To derive a consistent Monte Carlo estimator for the gradient of this latent-trajectory Bradley–Terry likelihood. This is necessary because the presence of latent CoT variables transforms the likelihood into a ratio of expectations, which cannot be optimized using standard techniques like Jensen-style bounds or typical RL objectives designed for verifiable rewards. This derivation is intended to yield a new optimization algorithm, termed Bradley–Terry Policy Optimization (BTPO).
3. **Empirical Validation:** To empirically demonstrate that the proposed BTPO framework enables stable and effective training of generative preference models that incorporate CoT reasoning. This includes showing that BTPO consistently outperforms prior heuristic approaches across multiple benchmarks and various model scales, thereby validating the necessity and effectiveness of a likelihood-grounded approach.

The overarching goal is to reconcile the statistical rigor of the Bradley–Terry maximum-likelihood objective with the expressive power of generative RL for complex reasoning tasks in a preference-based learning setting.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work lies in reformulating generative preference modeling to explicitly incorporate Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning as a latent variable within a Bradley–Terry (BT) statistical framework, and subsequently deriving an optimization method to handle the resulting complex likelihood.

#### 3.1. Dialog-based Generative Preference Model (GPM) Formulation

The authors introduce a dialog-based Generative Preference Model (GPM) structure to naturally expose the CoT token sequence as a latent variable. This GPM operates in a multi-turn dialog format:

1. **Response and Initial Prompt:** After an LLM generates a response (`y`) to an input prompt (`x`), the GPM is prompted to generate an intermediate "thought" (`o`) about the quality of `y`. This thought process `o` is a stochastic token sequence.
2. **Scoring Prompt:** Following the generated thought, the GPM is asked to rate the response, for instance, with a question like "Is this response good?". The probability of a specific target answer token (e.g., "Yes") – denoted `p(a | y, o)` – is then computed from the output logits of the LLM. This `p(a | y, o)` serves as the score for the response `y` conditioned on its generated thought `o`.

This setup ensures that the CoT sequence `o` is treated as an unobserved intermediate variable in the preference judgment process, aligning with the latent variable formulation.

#### 3.2. Bradley–Terry Likelihood with Latent CoT

In the standard Bradley–Terry model, the probability that a response `y+` is preferred over `y-` is a function of deterministic scores `exp(r*(y+)) / (exp(r*(y+)) + exp(r*(y-)))`. However, with latent CoT, the preference judgment depends on stochastically generated thoughts. The probability of preferring `y+` over `y-` (`π_ϕ(y+ ≻ y-)`) is expressed as:

`π_ϕ(y+ ≻ y-) = E_o+[p(a | y+, o+)] / (E_o+[p(a | y+, o+)] + E_o-[p(a | y-, o-)])`

where `o+ ~ π_ϕ(o | y+)` and `o- ~ π_ϕ(o | y-)` are the CoT sequences sampled for `y+` and `y-`, respectively, and `p(a | y, o)` is the probability of the positive preference judgment given the response `y` and its thought `o`. This formulation results in a likelihood that involves a logarithm of a ratio of expectations, posing a significant challenge for direct optimization using standard techniques.

#### 3.3. Bradley–Terry Policy Optimization (BTPO) Derivation

To address the challenge of optimizing this latent-trajectory BT likelihood, the authors derive a consistent Monte Carlo estimator for its gradient. The objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood: `l(ϕ) = -log π_ϕ(y+ ≻ y-)`.

The gradient `∇_ϕ l(ϕ)` is decomposed into two main components:

1. **Instance-Level Misalignment Weight:** The first part of the gradient is scaled by `p(y+ ≺ y-)`, which is `p- / (p+ + p-)`. This term acts as a dynamic weighting factor that assigns higher importance to response pairs where the model's estimated human preference probability is low, thus prioritizing "under-trained" or misaligned instances. This `p(y+ ≺ y-)` is estimated via Monte Carlo sampling.
2. **Policy Gradient for Expectations:** The second part involves estimating `∇_ϕ p / p` for both `p+` and `p-`. By using the identity `∇ log p = ∇p/p` and applying the policy gradient theorem to `∇_ϕ p = E_o∼π_ϕ(·|y) [π_ϕ(a | y, o) ∇_ϕ log π_ϕ(a, o | y)]`, the gradient term is approximated using Monte Carlo sampling:

 `∇_ϕ p / p ≈ Σ_i (˜ω_i [∇_ϕ log π_ϕ(a | y, o_i) + ∇_ϕ log π_ϕ(o_i | y)])`

 where `˜ω_i = π_ϕ(a|y,o_i) / Σ_j π_ϕ(a|y,o_j)` is a self-normalized conditional preference score. This term allows the gradient to update two aspects:
 - **Preference Scoring:** `∇_ϕ log π_ϕ(a | y, o_i)` encourages the model to assign a higher probability to the "Yes" token given the response and its thought.
 - **Thought Generation:** `∇_ϕ log π_ϕ(o_i | y)` encourages the generation of CoT sequences (`o_i`) that lead to correct preference judgments.

Combining these components, the final BTPO gradient `∇_ϕ l(ϕ)` integrates the misalignment weight with the policy gradient updates for both preference scoring and thought generation, where the thought generation is specifically guided by the normalized preference score as a reward, similar to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Benchmarks:** Three distinct preference modeling tasks were used:
 - **Helpfulness & Harmlessness (HH):** A subset of the Anthropic-HH dataset, multi-turn.
 - **Instruction Following (IF):** A subset of the ComplexIF dataset, multi-turn.
 - **Math Reasoning (Math):** Preference pairs generated from a finetuned Llemma-7B model, utilizing MetaMath for training and GSM8K/MATH500 for testing.
- **Baselines:**
 - Standard Bradley–Terry (BT) model.
 - GRAM (Generative Reward Model), a pairwise preference model without explicit thought generation.
 - Pairwise GPM trained with GRPO (representative of methods like J1, RM-R1, RRM).
 - Pointwise GPM trained with GRPO (representative of pointwise J1).
- **Metrics:** Classification accuracy on preference pairs, averaged over both input orders to mitigate bias.
- **Implementation:** Instruction-tuned LLMs (Qwen2.5-3B/7B-Instruct, Llama3.2-3B/Llama3.1-8B-Instruct) were used as backbones without additional supervised fine-tuning. BTPO utilized `n=4` thoughts per response, a rollout batch size of 256, a maximum of 1,000 generated tokens, a learning rate of 5e-7, and a training batch size of 32 for 2 epochs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of Bradley–Terry Policy Optimization (BTPO) yielded several key findings regarding its effectiveness and the limitations of prior heuristic approaches in generative preference modeling.

1. **Consistent Superiority of BTPO:** BTPO consistently outperformed all baseline methods across all three diverse preference modeling benchmarks: Helpfulness & Harmlessness (HH), Instruction Following (IF), and Math Reasoning.
 - Specific improvements were observed up to 4.8% on HH, 2.7% on IF, and 9.1% on Math Reasoning. These improvements were consistent across different model scales and backbones (Qwen2.5 and Llama3 variants). The detailed results showed BTPO consistently achieving the highest accuracy.
 - For instance, using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, BTPO achieved 70.4% on HH, 60.1% on IF, and 87.6% on Math, compared to the next best (BT or GRAM) at 68.7% (HH), 58.7% (IF), and 84.5% (Math).

2. **Instability and Inferiority of Heuristic RL GPMs:** Generative Preference Models (GPMs) trained with heuristic Reinforcement Learning (RL) formulations, specifically GRPO (both pairwise and pointwise), performed substantially worse than the standard Bradley–Terry (BT) model.
 - These GRPO-based methods often exhibited poor performance, with some instances showing accuracy near or at the 50% baseline (random guessing), indicating severe issues such as formatting errors or failure to learn meaningful preferences.
 - A direct comparison with an official RM-R1 checkpoint (a GRPO-based GPM) trained from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct demonstrated RM-R1 achieving only 62.7% on HH, 52.2% on IF, and 55.0% on Math Reasoning, even with both Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and RL. Further fine-tuning of RM-R1 on the specific datasets did not result in significant performance gains.
 - This finding suggests that simply reframing preference modeling as a generic generation task and applying standard RL objectives, without explicit grounding in a preference likelihood, is insufficient and can be detrimental to performance and stability.

3. **Comparison of Pointwise vs. Pairwise Baselines:** When comparing the standard BT model (a pointwise model that scores each response independently) and GRAM (a pairwise model that jointly evaluates two responses), it was observed that while GRAM occasionally performed marginally better on some tasks (e.g., HH), it consistently underperformed on tasks requiring more complex step-by-step reasoning, such as Instruction Following and Math Reasoning. This indicates that pairwise modeling, in general, may generalize less effectively on certain types of preference modeling tasks, potentially due to less frequent exposure during pretraining or inherent difficulty in jointly evaluating complex reasoning in a single pass.

4. **Ablation Studies Confirm BTPO Mechanisms:** Ablation studies, conducted using Llama3.2-3B-Instruct, provided insights into the contributions of BTPO's key components:
 - **Effectiveness of Generated Thoughts:** Comparing BTPO to "BT w/ prefilled thought" (where thoughts were generated offline and then a BT model was trained on these pre-existing thoughts) revealed that BTPO achieved significant gains. While pre-filled thoughts offered marginal improvements over standard BT, BTPO's ability to optimize thought generation *during training* led to consistently better performance. This indicates that BTPO effectively learns to generate informative CoT that enhances preference modeling.
 - **Importance of Misalignment Weight:** Removing the instance-level misalignment weight `ˆp(y+ ≺ y-)` from the BTPO objective resulted in substantial performance drops across HH and Math Reasoning tasks (e.g., from 70.5% to 59.1% on HH, and 81.6% to 62.1% on Math). This outcome underscores the critical role of incorporating the preference-modeling inductive bias into the training objective, highlighting that dynamic weighting of instances based on their current misalignment with human preferences is crucial for effective learning.

In summary, the results demonstrate that BTPO's statistically grounded approach, which explicitly accounts for CoT as a latent variable and uses a likelihood-consistent gradient, leads to superior and more stable performance in generative preference modeling compared to heuristic RL methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a notable contribution to the field of large language models (LLMs) and reinforcement learning, particularly in the domain of training models from human preferences. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Principled Integration of Reasoning and Preference Modeling:** The most significant contribution is the development of a statistically principled framework, Bradley–Terry Policy Optimization (BTPO), that effectively integrates Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning into generative preference models. By treating CoT as a latent variable within the Bradley–Terry likelihood, BTPO moves beyond heuristic reward optimization, providing a robust theoretical foundation for training LLMs on non-verifiable, preference-based tasks. This establishes a clearer methodological path for leveraging LLMs' reasoning capabilities in subjective evaluation contexts.

2. **Enhanced Performance and Stability:** Empirically, BTPO consistently outperforms existing heuristic RL-based approaches across various benchmarks and model scales. This not only demonstrates its practical efficacy but also addresses the reported issues of instability and suboptimal performance in prior generative preference models. The stable and effective training capabilities of BTPO suggest it can lead to more reliable and accurate LLM judges, which are critical for many downstream applications such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

3. **Addressing Limitations of Current RLHF Paradigms:** Current RLHF often relies on reward models that produce scalar scores, limiting their ability to capture and utilize explicit reasoning. By demonstrating how to effectively train LLMs to generate CoT alongside preference judgments directly from human preferences, BTPO offers a method to enrich reward modeling with transparent and interpretable reasoning processes. This could lead to more robust and aligned LLMs by improving the quality of the feedback signal.

4. **Advancement in Generative CoT Training:** The method enables the training of LLMs to generate effective CoT reasoning without requiring directly verifiable answers or pre-distilled thoughts from stronger, supervised models. This is particularly valuable for domains where verifiable answers are scarce or impossible to obtain, expanding the applicability of CoT training to a wider range of complex tasks.

5. **Broader Applicability to Latent Reasoning:** The core idea of treating text-based reasoning trajectories as unobserved intermediate variables within a likelihood-based objective is not limited to pairwise preferences. This perspective suggests a general framework for integrating generated reasoning into various probabilistic modeling tasks. Potential applications extend to other areas like structured prediction, complex decision-making, combinatorial optimization, and agentic reasoning systems where the intermediate thought processes are critical but remain latent. This could open new avenues for designing and training intelligent systems that exhibit and leverage explicit reasoning.

6. **Improved LLM Alignment and Trustworthiness:** By encouraging LLMs to generate coherent reasoning that aligns with human preferences, BTPO can contribute to developing LLMs that are not only more capable but also more transparent and trustworthy. The ability to inspect the generated thoughts leading to a preference judgment can help users understand and validate the model's decisions.

In conclusion, this research provides a significant step forward in training LLMs for complex, preference-driven tasks by offering a statistically sound and empirically effective method. Its impact is likely to be felt in improved LLM performance on subjective tasks, enhanced interpretability of preference models, and broader application of reasoning-based learning to scenarios lacking explicit ground truth.
