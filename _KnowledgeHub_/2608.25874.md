# Low-Resolution Perception for Robotic Packing

> [!summary] Summary
> An object-centric perception-planning pipeline for robotic packing using cheap, very low-resolution time-of-flight depth sensing. A density-aware Next Best View utility avoids redundant viewpoints, while an Object Manager tracks how often equivalent grasp hypotheses recur to score per-object stability and arbitrate pick-versus-view. Across 192 real grasp attempts it reached 65.6% success, grasping at only 40-60% surface reconstruction.

## Problem

- Classical packing work optimizes in-box placement while assuming high-resolution sensing and offline planning, so systems stay costly and hard to scale on lines.
- Standard Next Best View utilities score exploration gain only, so under sparse sensing neighboring poses look identical, producing redundant acquisitions and useless viewpoints.
- Prior grasp-aware view planners assume known object models, dense RGB-D quality, or accurate local surfaces, and address single targets rather than multi-object scheduling.

## Method

- **LR-NBV** utility sums an ==Exploration gain== over ray-validity-weighted traversed voxel volume with a ==Density gain== that inversely weights local point count, penalizing already-dense regions.
- ==Object Manager== builds object hypotheses by ==DBSCAN clustering== of the OctoMap point cloud into oriented bounding boxes, matching across frames by centroid displacement and box overlap for tracking.
- ==Stability-by-re-observation==: each grasp keeps a recurrence counter, score becomes quality times counter, summed per object; exceeding a threshold triggers pick, else Exploration or Exploitation picks the view target.

## Results

- LR-NBV needed significantly fewer poses than plain NBV (often under half, **p<0.05**) at comparable coverage, with significant IoU or bounding-box dimension gains on the tested objects.
- End-to-end, **126 of 192** attempts succeeded (**65.6%**); best cell was **83.3%** and worst **54.2%**, with mean surface reconstruction at grasp time held to **40-60%**.
- Stability-readiness errors were **33.3%** of the **66** failures; Exploitation cut them to **29.4%** versus **37.5%** for Exploration, and **30x30** input beat **60x60** on the small reflective part.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task-sufficient geometry beats complete reconstruction: partial 40-60% models suffice for grasping and container sizing if readiness is judged by evidence, not coverage.
> - Repeated agreement across independent observations is a cheap confidence signal; hypotheses that persist as the map improves are more trustworthy than one-shot scores.
> - More sensor resolution is not monotonically better: on reflective surfaces extra pixels add spurious returns, and downsampling acts as useful spatial smoothing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic packing is a critical task at the intersection of flexible manufacturing and modern logistics. It involves detecting items, estimating their dimensions, selecting and executing grasps, and placing objects into containers. Despite advancements in perception, this task remains challenging due to the high variability in object geometry and arrangement, which often limits the transferability of solutions across different applications. Historically, container selection and object arrangement have often relied on human tacit knowledge, leading to inconsistencies.

Prior research in robotic packing has frequently focused on maximizing in-box spatial efficiency. For example, Wang and Hauser (2019, 2021) investigated optimizing object placement under stability constraints for both known and unknown objects. These approaches commonly assume access to high-resolution sensing and rely on offline planning, which can limit their practical deployability in dynamic industrial settings. More recent efforts have aimed to reduce system complexity by exploring minimalistic grippers (Shome et al., 2019) and RGB cameras (Hong et al., 2020), indicating a trend towards more cost-effective and simpler setups.

This work addresses a specific gap within this landscape: investigating whether effective robotic packing necessitates detailed perception or if task-relevant information obtained from low-resolution sensing can suffice without compromising throughput and reliability. While low-resolution sensing has demonstrated utility in other domains such as safety (Ceriani et al., 2013), its application to object understanding for manipulation remains challenging due to limited detail and the requirement for multiple viewpoints. However, for robotic packing, full 3D reconstruction is often not strictly necessary; task-sufficient estimates of object dimensions are frequently adequate for container selection and placement. Previous studies have shown that robust manipulation can be supported by partial reconstructions (25–30% coverage) (Alliegro et al., 2022; Ten Pas et al., 2017).

The challenge intensifies in multi-object scenarios, where efficient decision-making regarding which object to scan next and when an object is ready for picking is crucial for maintaining high throughput. Traditional Next Best View (NBV) strategies, typically designed for high-fidelity reconstruction, often generate redundant poses and lack viewpoint diversity when applied to low-resolution data. Furthermore, dense but partial reconstructions can lead to grasp synthesis failures if they misrepresent the full object geometry.

This paper extends previous work by Preziosa et al. (2025) by proposing an end-to-end perception-planning pipeline specifically designed for multi-object robotic packing using low-cost, low-resolution depth sensors. It integrates an optimized low-resolution NBV (LR–NBV) strategy, which minimizes redundancy through density-aware exploration, into a broader framework that fuses grasp hypotheses to determine object readiness for picking or the need for further views. This places the work within the context of active perception, specifically focusing on NBV methods (Scott et al., 2003) and their adaptation to resource-constrained environments. While some prior work integrates grasp planning into view selection (Gualtieri and Platt, 2017; Arruda et al., 2016; Morrison et al., 2019), these often depend on extensive prior data, accurate local surface information, or high-quality RGB-D data. This research is more closely aligned with approaches like Breyer et al. (2022), which use closed-loop NBV to iteratively refine grasp hypotheses, but it expands this principle to multi-object scenarios with online estimation of object properties from minimal initial observations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a scalable perception framework for robotic packing tasks utilizing low-cost, low-resolution depth sensing. The work aims to demonstrate that effective robotic packing can be achieved without requiring high-fidelity object reconstruction, focusing instead on acquiring task-relevant geometric and grasp-related information.

The motivation for this research stems from several challenges and limitations in current robotic packing systems:
1. **Cost and Deployability:** Traditional robotic packing solutions often rely on expensive high-resolution sensors and computationally intensive offline planning, which limits their widespread adoption in cost-sensitive industrial and logistics environments. The motivation is to explore if low-cost sensors can provide sufficient information for practical deployment.
2. **Variability and Transferability:** The inherent variability in object geometry and arrangements within packing lines makes it difficult to develop broadly transferable robotic solutions. Existing methods often struggle with detecting and accurately estimating the size and pose of diverse items.
3. **Efficiency and Throughput:** In multi-object packing scenarios, making efficient decisions about which object to observe, from which viewpoint, and when an object is sufficiently understood for grasping is critical for maximizing throughput. Inefficient perception strategies, such as redundant views, directly translate to increased makespan.
4. **Limitations of Standard NBV for Low Resolution:** Conventional Next Best View (NBV) strategies are typically designed for detailed reconstruction and perform suboptimally with low-resolution data. Sparse observations can lead to similar utility scores for nearby viewpoints, resulting in redundant acquisitions and inefficient information gathering.
5. **Reliability of Grasp Hypotheses:** Partial or noisy reconstructions from low-resolution sensors can lead to unstable or unreliable grasp hypotheses. There is a need for a mechanism to assess the confidence in a grasp based on the evolving perceptual understanding of an object.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of the paper are:
- To propose a comprehensive framework where reconstruction cues dynamically guide the selection of the next best view (NBV).
- To integrate grasp evidence into a per-object stability estimate, enabling a joint decision-making process for determining whether to acquire more data or execute a grasp.
- To develop and incorporate a low-resolution NBV (LR–NBV) strategy that explicitly avoids redundant views while prioritizing the acquisition of task-relevant geometry. This strategy aims to overcome the limitations of traditional NBV in sparse data environments by balancing exploration with a preference for under-sampled regions.
- To minimize the total number of acquisitions, thereby reducing the makespan of the packing operation.
- To validate the proposed utility function of the LR–NBV strategy through an ablation study under severe low-resolution constraints.
- To conduct an end-to-end evaluation of the complete pipeline across different policies (exploration vs. exploitation) and sensing resolutions, demonstrating the practical applicability and scalability of low-resolution perception for robotic packing.
- To enhance the reliability of grasp execution by monitoring the consistency and re-occurrence of grasp proposals over time, treating stable, re-observed grasps as more trustworthy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology presents an end-to-end perception-planning pipeline designed for multi-object robotic packing using low-resolution depth sensors. The overarching goal is to minimize the number of sensor acquisitions, and consequently the makespan, by intelligently deciding at each iteration whether to refine an object's reconstruction (a "view" action) or to execute a grasp (a "pick" action).

The framework operates in a continuous loop, driven by an **Object Manager** that arbitrates between view and pick actions. This decision is informed by the current geometric state of the scene, represented by an OctoMap ($M_t$) and its derived point cloud ($P_t$), and by a set of current grasp hypotheses.

The pipeline comprises three main interacting modules:

1. **Grasp Generator (Sec. 3.1):**
 - **Functionality:** This module is activated whenever the occupancy map ($M_t$) is updated with new sensor data ($D(x_T)$). It takes the latest point cloud ($P_t$) as input.
 - **Output:** It generates a list of candidate grasps, $G_t = \{g_{t,1}, \ldots, g_{t,N_t}\}$, where each grasp $g_{t,m}$ includes a 6-DoF pose ($T_{t,m} \in \text{SE(3)}$) and an associated quality score ($q_{t,m} \in \mathbb{R}$).
 - **Mechanism:** The Grasp Generator can utilize either purely geometric algorithms or learning-based approaches. Its role is to estimate plausible gripper poses and assign a score reflecting grasp stability and feasibility, considering factors like antipodal consistency, collision risk derived from $M_t$, and robot reachability. This process runs at every map update to ensure the grasp hypotheses remain current with the evolving scene.

2. **Object Manager (Sec. 3.2):**
 - **Core Idea:** The Object Manager is central to the decision-making process. It employs a "stability by re-observation" principle, assuming that in low-resolution environments, early reconstructions may be partial, leading to unreliable local grasps. By tracking how grasp proposals evolve across iterations, the module prioritizes grasps that consistently reappear over time, considering them more trustworthy.
 - **Object Representation:** It maintains a set of identified objects $O_t = \{o^{(1)}_t, \ldots, o^{(N_t)}_t\}$. Each object $o^{(n)}_t$ is characterized by:
 - An oriented bounding box ($B^{(n)}_t$), defining its extent, pose, a dynamic region of interest (ROI) for view planning, and supporting collision checking.
 - A per-object grasp list ($G_{obj_t}(o^{(n)}_t)$), where each entry includes the grasp pose, quality score, an observation counter ($c_{o,j}$) tracking re-occurrences of equivalent grasps, and an updated grasp score ($\hat{s}_{o,j}$ that accounts for both current quality and the counter).
 - An object-level stability score ($S_t(o^{(n)}_t) \in \mathbb{R}$), used for the pick/view decision.
 - **Two Stages:**
 - **Object Identification:** Directly from the raw point cloud $P_t$, it segments the scene. Voxels in $M_t$ are classified as free, occupied, or uncertain. DBSCAN clustering is applied to group points, with each cluster representing an object hypothesis enclosed by an OBB. Temporal consistency is maintained by matching objects between $O_t$ and $O_{t-1}$ based on centroid displacement and OBB overlap, allowing tracking, detection of new objects, and occlusion handling.
 - **Pick-or-View Arbitration (Algorithm 1):**
 - Representative object poses are collected.
 - Each new incoming grasp from $G_t$ is associated with the nearest object (using a pose-distance criterion, $\tau_d$) and then matched against previously stored grasps for that object (using $\tau_g$).
 - If a match is found, the observation counter ($c$) for that grasp is incremented, and its stability score ($\hat{s}$) is updated (e.g., by multiplying quality score $q$ by counter $c$). If no match, a new grasp entry is added with an initial counter of 1.
 - The object-level stability $S_t(o)$ for each object is computed by aggregating the $\hat{s}$ scores of all its associated grasps.
 - **Decision Logic:** If any object's stability $S_t(o)$ exceeds a predefined threshold ($\tau_{stab}$), the Object Manager initiates a "pick" action, selecting the object with the highest stability. Otherwise, a "view" action is triggered.
 - **View Target Selection ($\pi_{view}$):** For a view action, the target object ($O_{view}$) is selected based on a policy: "Exploration" chooses the least-stable object (to spread sensing across objects), while "Exploitation" selects the most-stable object (to focus and trigger an early grasp). This chosen object then drives the LR–NBV module.

3. **Low Resolution Next Best View (LR–NBV) (Sec. 3.3):**
 - **Functionality:** This module implements the "view" action decided by the Object Manager. Given a target object $O_{view}$, it evaluates candidate sensor poses ($x_T$) to determine the most informative next acquisition.
 - **Candidate Poses:** Sampled on a viewing sphere centered at $O_{view}$.
 - **Utility Function $U(x_n)$:** The selection of the best pose is guided by a utility function tailored for low-resolution sensing, defined as a weighted sum of two gains: $U(x_n) = w_e G_e(x_T) + w_d G_d(x_T)$, where $w_e$ and $w_d$ are adjustable weights.
 - **Ray Casting:** Both gains rely on a ray-casting procedure that simulates the sensor's view from a candidate pose. A ray's validity ($V(r,t)$) is defined such that it is valid only if it intersects the object's ROI and is not occluded by the object itself, ensuring only visible portions contribute to reconstruction.
 - **Exploration Gain ($G_e(x_T)$):** This term maximizes the acquisition of novel information by prioritizing high-uncertainty areas. It sums the contributions of valid ray segments, weighted by their volumetric exploration gain (as in Selin et al., 2019).
 - **Density Gain ($G_d(x_T)$):** This gain specifically addresses the problem of redundancy in low-resolution sensing, where sparse observations can make nearby viewpoints appear similarly informative. It downweights rays passing through already dense areas and prioritizes under-sampled regions. Formally, $G_d(x_T) = \sum V(r,t_k) \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \rho(r,t_k)}$, where $\rho(r,t_k)$ is the number of object points within a spherical neighborhood around the ray segment $r(t_k)$. This term helps to reduce redundant acquisitions by encouraging exploration of less observed parts of the object.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of the proposed method involved two studies: an ablation study of the utility function and a full end-to-end pipeline evaluation.

#### 4.1. Study A: Utility Function Validation

This study assessed the effectiveness of the LR–NBV utility function under severe low-resolution constraints, extending prior work by Preziosa et al. (2025).
- **Experimental Setup:**
 - A UR5e manipulator equipped with a VL53L8CX time-of-flight sensor (8x8 pixel array, 45° FoV) was used.
 - Three methods were compared: LR–NBV (proposed), standard NBV (using only the Exploration gain, similar to classical reconstruction-oriented NBV), and a heuristic (HEU) that sampled 13 fixed viewpoints assuming known object pose.
 - Acquisition stopped when the marginal gain of the utility function dropped below a threshold ($\Delta_t \le \epsilon_{mg}$).
 - Four distinct objects (horizontal box, vertical box, gripper, flange) were reconstructed, with five runs per object.
 - Parameters included 50 candidate poses, $w_e=500, w_d=0.05$ for LR-NBV, and an OctoMap resolution of 0.01m.
- **Metrics:** Bounding box dimensions with Intersection-over-Union (IoU) (for reconstruction accuracy relevant to container size selection), number of poses (proxy for time efficiency), reconstructed surface coverage, and count of "useless poses" (viewpoints that did not increase coverage).
- **Key Findings:**
 - **Accuracy (IoU):** LR–NBV demonstrated statistically significant gains (p<0.05) in IoU for the "horizontal box" and "gripper" objects compared to baselines. For "vertical box" and "flange," significant differences (p<0.05) were observed in at least one bounding-box dimension, indicating more accurate size estimates in several instances.
 - **Efficiency (Number of Poses):** LR–NBV consistently required fewer poses (p<0.05) than standard NBV, often less than half, while maintaining comparable reconstructed surface coverage. This indicates improved efficiency in selecting informative viewpoints.
 - **Redundancy (Useless Poses):** Standard NBV exhibited more useless poses, which contributed to lower coverage and necessitated additional views. LR–NBV's density gain effectively reduced redundancy.

#### 4.2. Study B: Full Pipeline Evaluation

This study evaluated the end-to-end robotic packing pipeline under realistic operating conditions, focusing on the Object Manager's pick/view arbitration.
- **Experimental Setup:**
 - An ABB GoFa 12 collaborative arm with a MaixSense A010 time-of-flight sensor (100x100 resolution, 70°x60° FoV) was used. Sensor inputs were downsampled to effective resolutions of $R_{60}$ (60x60) and $R_{30}$ (30x30).
 - Grasp generation was performed by GraspNet (Fang et al., 2020) in a depth-only configuration.
 - Two view policies for the Object Manager were compared: "Exploration" (XPLR), which targets the least-stable object, and "Exploitation" (XPLT), which targets the most-stable object.
 - Three objects (two types: Obj A and Obj B, representing production parts with different sizes and reflective properties) were randomly placed on a pallet. The robot transferred them sequentially to a container. A single initial randomized acquisition was performed.
 - A 2x2 experimental design (R60/R30 resolutions combined with XPLT/XPLR policies) was tested across the two object types, resulting in 16 trials per setting (8 per object type) for a total of 64 trials and 192 grasp attempts (no re-attempts).
- **Metrics:** Grasp success rate (%), mean surface reconstruction (% coverage) at grasp time, failure breakdown by cause, and makespan (analyzed through acquisition curves).
- **Key Findings:**
 - **Grasp Success Rate:** An overall grasp success rate of 65.6% (126/192 attempts) was achieved.
 - For Obj A (larger part), higher resolution ($R_{60}$) generally led to better grasp success (e.g., R60-XPLT: 70.8%). Denser and more coherent geometry improved pose estimation and grasp ranking.
 - For Obj B (smaller, shiny part), lower resolution ($R_{30}$) often outperformed $R_{60}$ (e.g., R30-XPLR: 83.3%). Higher resolution sometimes captured more false reflections, creating spurious points that confused clustering, whereas lower resolution provided a mild spatial smoothing effect, yielding more reliable grasps.
 - **Surface Reconstruction at Grasp Time:** The mean surface reconstruction coverage at the point of grasp decision consistently fell within the 40–60% range across all policy-resolution pairs and object types. This aligns with the design goal of acquiring partial, task-sufficient geometry rather than exhaustive reconstruction, while avoiding critically under-reconstructed scenarios.
 - **Grasp Failures Breakdown:** Out of 66 failures, "Stability-readiness failures" (STAB), where a grasp was deemed ready but failed due to unreconstructed essential regions, accounted for 33.3% of all failures. XPLT exhibited a lower percentage of STAB failures (29.4%) compared to XPLR (37.5%), suggesting that XPLT's focused approach might enforce stricter verification of grasp readiness. "Spurious point" failures (SPUR), often due to sensor noise amplified by reflective surfaces, were identified as a significant error source. The results indicated that the stability index, despite the challenges of low-resolution reconstruction, remained effective for grasp decision-making.
 - **Acquisition Curves (Makespan):** The number of observations required before each grasp showed that $R_{30}$ configurations generally required more acquisitions than $R_{60}$, which is consistent with the higher information content per view at higher resolution. A sublinear progression was observed, meaning that subsequent objects (Object #2, #3) typically required fewer *additional* observations because earlier views for previous objects had already provided some relevant information. The XPLR policy tended to follow a clearer logarithmic pattern, while XPLT was closer to linear due to its focus on one object at a time. The presence of reflective areas with low-resolution sensing could intermittently corrupt reconstructions, sometimes leading to partial "tear-down" and potentially making the makespan for smaller objects comparable to larger ones.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates that low-resolution, low-cost perception can be a viable and practical solution for robotic packing, challenging the conventional reliance on high-fidelity sensing. The findings suggest that effective robotic packing does not necessarily require full 3D geometry but rather task-relevant information combined with an adaptive sensing strategy.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:
- **Reduced Hardware Costs:** By proving the efficacy of low-resolution depth sensors, the research enables the use of more affordable sensing hardware. This can significantly lower the overall capital investment for robotic packing systems, making automation more accessible to a wider range of industries, particularly small and medium-sized enterprises.
- **Increased System Scalability and Throughput:** The proposed object-centric pipeline, with its LR–NBV strategy and grasp-driven reconstruction, contributes to more efficient information gathering by actively avoiding redundant views and focusing on informative regions. This efficiency, combined with intelligent pick-or-view arbitration, can lead to reduced makespan and higher throughput in robotic packing operations, which is crucial for modern logistics and manufacturing.
- **Enhanced Robustness with Limited Data:** The "stability by re-observation" criterion addresses a key challenge of low-resolution sensing: the inherent unreliability of initial grasp hypotheses derived from partial or noisy data. By favoring grasps that consistently reappear and improve in confidence over time, the system enhances the robustness of grasp execution under perceptual uncertainty.
- **Adaptive and Task-Focused Perception:** The framework's ability to dynamically switch between viewing (for reconstruction refinement) and picking (for task execution) based on real-time object stability estimates represents an adaptive approach to perception. This ensures that sensing resources are strategically deployed where they offer the most benefit, either to improve understanding of uncertain objects or to capitalize on grasp-ready items.
- **Validation of Partial Reconstruction Sufficiency:** The consistent finding that grasp success is achieved with only 40-60% surface reconstruction coverage reinforces the notion that full object geometry is often superfluous for manipulation tasks. This paradigm shift can simplify perception requirements and reduce computational overhead.
- **Future Research Directions:** The identification of sensor noise, especially from reflective surfaces, as a major error source opens clear avenues for future research. This includes developing strategies to identify and efficiently sense problematic regions, potentially through multi-modal sensing or advanced signal processing techniques tailored for challenging materials.
- **Industrial Applicability:** This work has direct relevance for smart production and logistics. It offers a pathway to more flexible, automated manufacturing systems that can handle diverse product geometries with reduced hardware complexity and improved operational efficiency, thereby contributing to the advancement of Robotics in Manufacturing Systems.
