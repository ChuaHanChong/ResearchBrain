# Mode-Conditioning Unlocks Superior Test-Time Scaling

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University introduce Mode-Conditioning (ModC), a framework for large language models that explicitly allocates test-time compute across distinct reasoning strategies. ModC consistently improved Pass@k scaling on various benchmarks, achieving up to 4x inference efficiency on long Chain-of-Thought math problems and boosting Pass@k by up to 20% on adversarial graph-search tasks.

## Problem

- Parallel sampling in large language models (LLMs) for reasoning tasks suffers from "diversity collapse" during post-training processes like supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL).
- Diversity collapse causes diminishing returns for increased test-time compute, as additional samples frequently reproduce similar errors or generate indistinguishable solutions, limiting Pass@k improvements.
- Standard training methods often underutilize the inherent diversity in training data, failing to maintain distinct successful reasoning modes within the model's generation capabilities.

## Method

- The paper introduces **Mode-Conditioning** (ModC), a paradigm that explicitly structures test-time scaling by enforcing coverage across multiple distinct reasoning strategies.
- Two primary training methods are developed: training separate specialist models for each reasoning mode or training a single model with mode-specific prefixes to control distinct strategies at inference.
- Automated mode discovery using gradient clustering is proposed and validated, enabling ModC to identify and leverage implicit modes in datasets lacking explicit annotations.

## Results

- ModC achieved up to **20%** improvement in Pass@1024 on adversarial graph-search tasks and up to **15%** Pass@k gains on math reasoning benchmarks compared to standard training.
- ModC demonstrated substantial efficiency gains, matching the Pass@1024 of standard training with only **25%** of the samples (k=256 vs. k=1024) for long Chain-of-Thought reasoning, representing an approximate **4x** speedup.
- Automated mode discovery via gradient clustering effectively identified underlying modes, achieving a **98.7%** F1 score in recovering ground-truth teacher assignments and enabling ModC to deliver similar performance benefits as explicit mode labels.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly allocating test-time compute across distinct reasoning modes is theoretically superior to sampling from a model's uncertain mixture distribution, especially when mode success probabilities differ.
> - Standard fine-tuning often leads to diversity collapse, underutilizing diverse training data and failing to effectively leverage distinct problem-solving strategies from multiple teachers or algorithmic approaches.
> - ModC can effectively recover and leverage implicit reasoning modes within datasets, even without explicit labels, through techniques like gradient clustering, demonstrating untapped potential in existing data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has increasingly emphasized test-time compute scaling as a critical factor for achieving advanced reasoning capabilities. Two primary methods for scaling test-time compute are lengthening individual reasoning traces and parallel sampling. Parallel sampling, which involves generating multiple independent attempts to solve a problem, has demonstrated effectiveness, particularly in domains where solutions can be automatically verified, such as mathematics, coding, and scientific discovery.

However, a significant limitation to the effectiveness of parallel sampling is the phenomenon of "diversity collapse." This occurs during post-training processes like supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL), where LLMs tend to converge on a narrow set of dominant reasoning strategies or modes. Consequently, additional samples often reproduce similar errors or generate indistinguishable solutions, leading to diminishing returns as computational resources for parallel sampling are increased. This issue reduces the efficacy of metrics like Pass@k, which measure the probability of at least one out of *k* samples being correct.

Prior research has explored various approaches to mitigate diversity collapse and improve parallel test-time scaling. These include modifications to beam search algorithms to explicitly optimize for diversity, the use of diverse prompting strategies, methods for optimizing directly for best-of-k performance during fine-tuning (e.g., Inference-aware fine-tuning, Bond), techniques like ensembling fine-tuned weights with base models to retain diversity, and pretraining with logit distillation.

This work introduces Mode-Conditioning (ModC) as a complementary paradigm. Instead of solely modifying the training objective to prevent collapse or altering sampling strategies, ModC explicitly structures test-time scaling by enforcing coverage across different reasoning strategies. It positions itself as a data-centric conditioning approach to the fine-tuning process, aiming to explicitly encode specialist modes within the model. While conceptually simple, its implementation draws inspiration from techniques like steering model behavior via explicit conditioning tokens, seen in controlled text generation and instruction tuning, and has parallels with specialized model architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), though ModC differentiates by processing all data points through the entire model rather than routing to subparts. The paper also explores automated mode discovery through gradient clustering, connecting to research on understanding training dynamics and data selection. By focusing on actively leveraging and controlling distinct reasoning modes, ModC aims to unlock the full potential of diverse training data that standard training methods may underutilize.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observed limitation of parallel sampling in LLMs: **diversity collapse**. Despite the theoretical benefits of drawing multiple samples to increase the chance of success (measured by Pass@k), practical implementations of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) often lead models to concentrate on a limited set of reasoning strategies. This means that successive samples frequently yield the same errors or highly correlated solutions, causing the Pass@k metric to plateau and limiting the efficiency gains from increased test-time compute. The underlying problem is that standard training underutilizes the inherent diversity present in training data, failing to maintain distinct successful reasoning modes within the model's generation capabilities.

The primary objective of this work is to address this challenge by introducing **mode-conditioning (ModC)**. ModC proposes a novel paradigm that explicitly structures test-time scaling around multiple, distinct reasoning modes. The core hypothesis is that by deliberately allocating test-time compute across these diverse strategies, the model can reliably access different problem-solving approaches, thereby improving the probability of success, especially on problems where a dominant mode might fail but a less probable one could succeed.

Specifically, the key objectives are:

1. **Develop a principled framework for explicit mode allocation:** The paper aims to formalize how explicit allocation of samples across different modes (e.g., k/2 samples for mode 1, k/2 for mode 2) can theoretically outperform drawing k samples from a single, potentially collapsed, mixed distribution.
2. **Design scalable training methods for ModC:** Recognizing the unreliability of ad-hoc prompting for mode control, the research seeks to develop robust training objectives that enable explicit control over modes at inference time. Two primary instantiations are explored:
 - **Training separate specialist models:** Each model is fine-tuned exclusively on data corresponding to a specific reasoning mode. This ensures strong specialization.
 - **Training a single model with mode-specific prefixes:** A single model is trained to associate distinct prefixes (e.g., `[Mode 1]`, `[Mode 2]`) with different reasoning strategies, allowing for knowledge sharing while maintaining mode control.
3. **Validate ModC on controlled tasks:** Evaluate the effectiveness of ModC in a controlled environment where reasoning modes are clearly defined and detectable (e.g., BFS and DFS in a search problem). This allows for a precise analysis of mode coverage and its impact on test-time scaling.
4. **Demonstrate ModC's benefits on real-world reasoning benchmarks:** Apply ModC to complex, large-scale reasoning tasks, including short and long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning in mathematics, using distillation from multiple teacher models with distinct problem-solving styles. The goal is to show consistent Pass@k improvements and efficiency gains across various model sizes and families.
5. **Enable ModC without explicit mode labels:** Address the practical challenge that most real-world datasets lack explicit mode annotations. The objective is to develop and validate an automated method, specifically gradient clustering, for discovering and labeling modes implicitly present in diverse training data, thereby making ModC broadly applicable.
6. **Assess ModC's compatibility and impact on reinforcement learning:** Investigate whether ModC's benefits for diversity and test-time scaling persist or can be further enhanced when integrated with reinforcement learning pipelines, including both standard RL and diversity-inducing RL methods.

Ultimately, the motivation is to provide a simple yet effective remedy for unlocking the full benefits of diversity in test-time scaling, challenging the notion that standard training adequately utilizes diverse data and offering a pathway to more reliable and efficient frontier reasoning systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the **Mode-conditioning (ModC) framework** as a method to enhance test-time scaling by explicitly managing reasoning modes.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Framework

The paper begins by mathematically formalizing the advantage of ModC. For an input *x*, Pass@k_std (standard parallel sampling) is defined as `1 - (1 - p_x)^k`, where `p_x` is the single-sample success probability. In contrast, for two modes with success probabilities `p_1,x` and `p_2,x`, ModC with an even split of *k/2* samples per mode yields `Pass@k_ModC(x) = 1 - (1 - p_1,x)^(k/2) * (1 - p_2,x)^(k/2)`. Through Jensen's inequality, the paper demonstrates that `Pass@k_ModC(x)` is strictly greater than `E_w_x[Pass@k_std(x; w_x)]` where `w_x` is the model's uncertain probability of using the first mode, even when `E[w_x] = 1/2`. This theoretical argument establishes that explicit allocation consistently outperforms sampling from a single, uncertain mixture when mode success probabilities differ.

#### 3.2. Mode-Conditioned Training Methods

To implement explicit mode control at test time, two training methodologies are explored:
- **Mode-conditioned training with separate models:** The training data is partitioned based on known reasoning modes. A distinct specialist model is then trained independently on each mode's subset of the data. At inference, the total sampling budget *k* is evenly divided among these specialist models. This approach promotes strong specialization but limits knowledge sharing.
- **Mode-conditioned training with prefixes:** A single model is trained to learn multiple modes by prepending unique discrete condition tokens (e.g., `[Mode 1]`, `[Mode 2]`) to the input prompt for each respective mode during training. At inference, samples are drawn by cycling through these mode-specific prefixes, ensuring balanced allocation. This method allows for knowledge transfer across modes within a single model.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup and Tasks

- **Controlled Graph-Search (Countdown Task):**
 - **Task Description:** A variant of the Game of 24, requiring arithmetic operations on starting numbers to reach a target. It naturally admits two distinct modes: Breadth-First Search (BFS) and Depth-First Search (DFS) with heuristic pruning.
 - **Data Generation:** Training data (163K problems, 97K DFS, 65K BFS solutions) was generated using rejection sampling to ensure solvability by at least one oracle algorithm. Test sets included a "natural" set (500 random problems) and an "adversarial" set (problems where either oracle BFS or DFS, but not both, achieved <5% success rate), specifically designed to highlight the need for mode diversity.
 - **Models:** Qwen2.5-Base models (0.5B to 7B parameters) were trained for 4 epochs.
 - **Analysis:** Focused on the fraction of BFS used by models for each test problem and Pass@k metrics on both natural and adversarial test sets.

- **Large-Scale Reasoning Benchmarks (Math Post-Training):**
 - **Distillation from Multiple Teachers:** Used two distinct teacher models (DeepSeek-R1 and GPT-OSS-120B for short CoT; QwQ-32B and DeepSeek-R1 for long CoT) to generate diverse CoT reasoning traces.
 - **Training Strategies:** Four strategies were compared:
 1. **Mixed teachers:** All teacher data combined and used for standard SFT.
 2. **Best teacher:** SFT using data from the teacher yielding the highest baseline performance.
 3. **ModC with separate models:** Separate models trained on each teacher's data.
 4. **ModC with prefixes:** Single model trained with teacher identity tokens as prefixes.
 - **Short Chain-of-Thoughts (Short CoT):**
 - **Dataset:** NuminaMath (Li et al., 2024).
 - **Evaluation:** MATH500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021) using Pass@k.
 - **Models:** Qwen2.5-Base (0.5B–7B) and OLMo2-Base (1B–7B) models, trained for 4 epochs with AdamW.
 - **Long Chain-of-Thoughts (Long CoT):**
 - **Dataset:** Subset of OpenThoughts-3 (Guha et al., 2025).
 - **Evaluation:** AIME 2025 using Pass@k.
 - **Models:** Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, fine-tuned.
 - **Data Scale Ablations:** Experiments on OpenThoughts-3 with varying data scales (5k, 10k, 20k samples).

#### 3.4. Automated Mode Discovery with Gradient Clustering

- **Motivation:** To apply ModC when explicit mode labels are unavailable.
- **Method:** For each training example `(x, y)`, compute gradients `g_θ(x, y) = ∇_θ log p_θ(y|x)`. These high-dimensional gradient vectors are then reduced in dimensionality using Rademacher random projection. Finally, the projected vectors are clustered into *C* groups, with each cluster representing an inferred reasoning "mode."
- **Validation:** Tested on the multi-teacher short CoT dataset to see if clusters aligned with actual teacher identities.
- **Application:** Applied to NuminaMath without prior mode labels to discover and condition on implicit modes.

#### 3.5. Integration with Reinforcement Learning (RL)

- **Methods:** Investigated ModC's benefits when followed by (1) standard RL and (2) Pass@k RL (Chen et al., 2025b), which aims to prevent diversity collapse during RL.
- **Initialization:** Policies were initialized from either standard SFT models or ModC SFT models (specifically the separate models variant due to its simplicity in this context).
- **Evaluation:** Pass@k scores on MATH500.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings demonstrating the efficacy and applicability of the Mode-Conditioning (ModC) framework.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Advantage of ModC

The paper establishes a theoretical basis for ModC, showing that explicitly allocating compute evenly across distinct modes is strictly superior to sampling from a model's uncertain mixture distribution, particularly when mode success probabilities are not equal. This advantage holds even if the model's inherent mode-usage is unbiased in expectation.

#### 4.2. Controlled Graph-Search (Countdown Task)

- **Diversity Collapse in Standard Training:** Experiments on the Countdown task demonstrated that models trained with standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on mixed DFS and BFS data often fail to balance the two algorithms, predominantly using one strategy for many test problems. This imbalance persists even with a 50-50 distribution of DFS and BFS in the training data (Figure 2).
- **ModC Achieves Balanced Allocation:** In contrast, ModC, using both separate specialist models and mode-specific prefixes, successfully balanced test-time compute allocation, with the fraction of BFS samples per problem concentrating around 0.5 (Figure 2).
- **Improved Pass@k Scaling:** ModC consistently improved Pass@k scaling compared to standard training. On the natural test set, gains were up to 8% for Pass@1024, maintained across model sizes (0.5B-3B). On the adversarial test set, designed to require mode diversity, the gains were even more pronounced, boosting Pass@1024 by up to 20% (Figure 3).
- **Comparison of ModC Variants:** On Countdown, ModC with separate models generally outperformed ModC with prefixes, suggesting that for this specific task, strong specialization without knowledge sharing across modes was more beneficial.
- **Ablation Studies:** Randomly partitioning training data or ensuring balanced DFS/BFS distribution in standard training did not yield comparable gains to ModC, highlighting the importance of mode-aware conditioning (Figure 4).

#### 4.3. Math Post-Training (Short and Long Chain-of-Thoughts)

- **Ineffectiveness of Naive Teacher Mixture:** On both short- and long-form CoT reasoning, naively mixing data from multiple teacher models typically underperformed or was at best comparable to fine-tuning on only the best-performing single teacher. This indicates that standard SFT struggles to leverage diverse teacher strategies effectively (Figures 5 and 6).
- **ModC Unlocks Superior Scaling:** ModC training consistently unlocked superior test-time scaling across various model families (Qwen2.5, OLMo2) and scales (0.5B-7B).
 - **Short CoT (NuminaMath on MATH500):** ModC yielded up to 10% gains on Qwen2.5-0.5B and 15% on OLMo2-7B in Pass@k (Figure 5). ModC with prefixes generally outperformed separate models, suggesting that knowledge sharing across modes is crucial for complex math reasoning.
 - **Long CoT (OpenThoughts-3 on AIME 2025):** ModC provided substantial efficiency gains, matching the Pass@1024 of standard training with only k=256 samples (an approximate 4x improvement in inference efficiency), while also achieving a higher maximum attainable Pass@k (Figures 1 and 6).
- **Consistency Across Data Scales:** ModC demonstrated consistent benefits across varying data scales (5k to 20k samples) in OpenThoughts-3, with the most significant gains observed at smaller data scales (Figure 10).

#### 4.4. Automated Mode Discovery with Gradient Clustering

- **Recovery of Teacher Identity:** Gradient clustering effectively identified underlying modes, achieving a 98.7% F1 score in recovering ground-truth teacher assignments on multi-teacher data (Section 5.2).
- **Effective Automated ModC:** ModC applied with these automated, gradient-discovered clusters yielded nearly identical test-time scaling benefits as ModC using explicit teacher labels (Figure 7).
- **Gains on General Data:** When applied to NuminaMath, a dataset without explicit mode labels, ModC with gradient clustering consistently improved Pass@k scores compared to standard training across various model scales (Figure 8). This indicates that standard training leaves substantial performance untapped by failing to fully exploit diverse data, which ModC addresses even without prior mode knowledge.

#### 4.5. Integration with Reinforcement Learning

- **Improved Pass@k Post-RL:** Even when standard RL brought both standard SFT and ModC SFT models to comparable Pass@1 scores, ModC models (specifically the separate models variant) consistently exhibited higher Pass@k scores for k ≥ 2 (Figure 9).
- **Enhanced Diversity-Inducing RL:** ModC further boosted the performance of Pass@k RL, an intervention designed to explicitly prevent diversity collapse during RL training. This suggests ModC successfully enriches the solution space without degrading the accuracy of the top output and complements existing diversity-enhancing RL methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Mode-conditioning (ModC) framework offers a significant contribution to the field of large language model (LLM) reasoning and test-time compute scaling, with several potential impacts:

1. **Addressing Diversity Collapse:** The most direct significance of ModC is its ability to directly mitigate the problem of diversity collapse, a widely recognized limitation of parallel sampling in LLMs. By explicitly allocating test-time compute across distinct reasoning modes, ModC provides a principled mechanism to ensure that models explore a broader range of solution strategies, thereby overcoming the diminishing returns observed in standard parallel inference.

2. **Enhanced Efficiency and Performance:** The demonstration of substantial efficiency gains (e.g., up to 4x faster inference for comparable performance on long CoT tasks) and higher maximum attainable Pass@k scores highlights ModC's potential to make LLM reasoning systems more resource-efficient and capable. This is particularly relevant as reasoning tasks become more complex and the computational cost of LLM inference continues to be a concern.

3. **Unlocking Untapped Data Potential:** The findings suggest that standard training methodologies often underutilize the inherent diversity within training data. ModC provides a framework to effectively harness this diversity, transforming varied training examples (e.g., from multiple teachers or different algorithmic approaches) into distinct, controllable reasoning modes. This implies that existing datasets, even those not explicitly curated for mode diversity, may contain untapped potential that ModC can extract.

4. **Broad Applicability:** ModC's effectiveness across various model sizes (0.5B to 7B), model families (Qwen2.5, OLMo2), and task types (controlled graph search, short and long CoT math reasoning) indicates its generalizability. Its applicability to both explicit mode labels (like teacher identities or search algorithms) and implicitly discovered modes via gradient clustering expands its utility to real-world datasets where explicit mode annotations are often absent.

5. **Complementary to Existing Methods:** The research demonstrates that ModC's benefits extend to and can further enhance reinforcement learning (RL) techniques, including those specifically designed to promote diversity (e.g., Pass@k RL). This suggests ModC is not a replacement but a complementary intervention that can be integrated into existing and future LLM training pipelines to improve their performance and robustness.

6. **Guidance for Future LLM Development:** ModC introduces a new lens through which to consider the design and training of reasoning-focused LLMs. It encourages future research into identifying, characterizing, and controlling diverse reasoning strategies. Potential future directions, such as extending ModC to a larger number of modes, richer behavioral dimensions (e.g., reasoning depth), and adaptive allocation policies, indicate a fertile ground for further innovation in building more reliable and powerful AI systems capable of complex problem-solving. The focus on balanced modes in RL could also inform strategies for diverse exploration in reinforcement learning.
